<Section id="s4" no="4" title="Tagging: wear the product hat" crumb="4 Tagging"

  meta="About 25 min · class 01:40–02:03">

Gravatar is designed and served. The next feature arrives with nothing else attached.

This chapter is about the questions the brief hides: who may tag whom, how many tags a photo carries, who gets told, which other teams the feature leans on, and where on the picture a tag sits.

<Section id="s4-1" no="4.1" title="One line of brief, three hats" crumb="4.1 Three hats"
  dek="Three hats at once: the product manager’s, the architect’s and the engineer’s. This chapter mostly wears the first."
  meta="About 4 min · class 01:40–01:43">

Picture a startup. One day a CXO comes over and says: “Let’s allow people to tag people in photos.” That’s all. That one sentence is your entire product briefing, and you are expected to go and build it. [[L-0176]]

The tagging system itself is easy, so this chapter doesn’t design it. It uses it to show something harder: how one simple system affects many others. A senior engineer is responsible for more than the one system they build. They have to see how it interacts with the systems around it, and how it could affect them. From here on the course puts you in exactly those situations: several hats, several discussions at once, extensibility, and work that crosses teams. [[L-0174 L-0175 L-0004]]

Often the people who hand you the sentence haven’t thought it through. That is more common than you’d think. They trust your instinct, so it falls to you to think of what they haven’t. The high level is still the easy part. The hard part is how the feature fits into the larger puzzle. Given one line, your job is to dissect it, go into the details and point them out. [[L-0177 L-0199]]

So the first pass is about requirements only, with no solution at all. Brainstorm the questions you would ask yourself and your team, not the manager, and take them one at a time. The first instinct, “what should the schema for tags be?”, is the wrong first question. Nothing about engineering yet: all you have is one line, so think on the product side. [[L-0178 L-0183 L-0184]]

Thinking like a product person doesn’t mean you stop being an engineer. A senior engineer wears three hats, and wears all three at once, not one after another. The **product manager’s** hat is for requirements: the things others haven’t thought about, and bringing clarity. The **architect’s** hat designs the system with its future extensibility in mind. The **engineer’s** hat goes into the low-level, nitty-gritty details and makes sure the system is optimal across every layer. Worn together, they make the synergy. The course comes back to this in the next two weeks. [[L-0179 L-0180 L-0181 L-0182]]

On the class board the questions went up in this order: who can tag (authorization); max limit of people tagged in a photo; notification & throttling; self-removal; face recognition & suggestion (SLA); profile/activity (DB); feed. <Section id="s4-2" no="4.2" title="Who can tag me?" crumb="4.2 Who can tag me"
  dek="The obvious check is who may tag a photo. The question that changes the design is who may tag you."
  meta="About 3 min · class 01:43–01:46 and 01:52–01:53">

Someone you barely know tags you in a photo. Should they be able to?

One proposal is that only the photo’s owner can tag people in it, so nobody tags the wrong people or objects. That is an obvious check, and you would build it, but it isn’t the interesting question, and neither is whether a tag is correct. The question that matters runs the other way: who can tag *me* in their photos? Whether I allow it should be in my control. [[L-0185]]

Saying yes to that control means tagging needs a separate authorization layer, one that keeps track of who is allowed to tag whom. You see this day in, day out on Instagram, and on Twitter, where some people can’t be tagged in your posts. A profile setting, “can anyone tag me?”, is the same requirement seen from the settings page: it is a switch in this layer. [[L-0186 L-0200]]

Then self-removal. If people tag you in, say, an obscene photo, you must be able to remove yourself from it. That makes authorization more interesting, and more granular. The photo belongs to user 1, yet you, a different user, are allowed to change it by taking your own tag off. It is a little more complex than it first looked, and it needs a really robust authorization layer. [[L-0201 L-0202]]

<Section id="s4-3" no="4.3" title="How many tags, and who gets told" crumb="4.3 Limits and notifications"
  dek="Five tags or ten thousand picks the schema. A yes to notifications pulls in another team, and a kind of throttling no load test will ask for."
  meta="About 7 min · class 01:46–01:52, and 00:02–00:05">

A photo from a 40-person team offsite. How many people can one photo carry?

The next requirement is the maximum number of people tagged in one photo. Keep it per photo, not per album or collection. [[L-0187]]

A tempting worry is that a limit of five means managing several people adding tags to one photo at the same time. It doesn’t: only the owner tags people in their own photo, and nobody else can add tags to it, so concurrency isn’t the challenge here. [[L-0188]] The real question is what the number does to the system.

<PauseAndThink k="L-0188 L-0189 L-0190 L-0191"
  q="Product asks you to decide the maximum number of people tagged in one photo. Does it matter to the design whether the answer is 5 or 10,000?"
  choices={[{ label: 'No: it’s a validation rule, so just check the number' }, { label: 'Yes: it changes how tags are stored and served', correct: true }]}
  right="Right: the limit decides the schema and the API."
  wrong="Not quite: it does matter. It changes storage and the API, not just validation.">

Five fits in an array inside the photo’s record. Ten thousand needs one entry per tag, because a document that big is costly to rewrite and to read, and a paginated endpoint, because nobody wants 10,000 names at once. Every requirement question should change the design; that is why it was worth asking. Concurrency isn’t the issue, because only the owner tags their own photo.

</PauseAndThink>

Take the two extremes. With a limit of five, keep the tagged people in an array, directly in the photo’s document. With 10,000 they need separate entries. Kept together, that one document grows really big, the problem from <LectureLink lec="lec09">Lecture 7</LectureLink>. And nobody goes through 10,000 names in one shot, so you would serve them through paginated endpoints. The limit decides your storage schema. [[L-0189 L-0190]] For scale: MongoDB caps one document at 16 MiB <Cite href="https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/reference/limits/">mongodb.com</Cite>, and 10,000 user ids fit well under that. So the practical reasons for separate entries are the cost of rewriting and reading one ever-growing document, and pagination, rather than the hard limit.

### Who gets told [[rail: Notifications]]

Should the tagged person be notified? Yes. Otherwise someone could tag you in a really bad photo and you would never know. [[L-0192]]

That yes has a cost outside your team. Before rollout, talk to the notification team. People are very likely to tag each other, so the moment the feature launches they will see a huge spike in traffic, and they need to get ready to scale up. If they aren’t ready and you start pushing tag notifications at them, their system gets overburdened and other critical notifications can be affected. The answer to “do we notify?” is yes in most products, so this conversation is nearly always yours to start. [[L-0193 L-0194]]

It also brings throttling, of a different kind: product-level throttling. Users hate apps that over-notify. By the instructor’s rule of thumb, good apps send only about three to five notifications a week. An app that sends you one every day is a big turn-off, and people are likely to uninstall it. A notification for every single photo you are tagged in is a bad experience. [[L-0195 L-0196]]

So ask the notification team whether they throttle, and if they don’t, add it. Don’t bombard people with one notification per tagged photo: club them, and give a rich notification instead. Think of a very famous celebrity. One photo goes up, lots of people tag them in it, and without clubbing they get a flood. [[L-0197 L-0198]] Mobile platforms support the clubbing. On Android, related notifications can be put in one group with a summary (Android 7.0 and later), the system may group an app’s notifications by itself once it posts four or more, and the docs suggest updating an existing notification instead of posting new ones <Cite href="https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/notifications/group">developer.android.com</Cite>.

<Figure id="fig-4d" no="Fig 4·d" k="L-0195 L-0197 L-0198"
  caption="One notification per tag floods a celebrity tagged many times in one photo. Clubbed, the same tags become one rich notification. New figure.">
  <Desk><OnePerTagOrClubbed /></Desk>
  <Phone><OnePerTagOrClubbed layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

<MarginNote title="How notification systems fan out" why="skip unless you build notifications" k="L-0006 L-0007 L-0008 L-0009 L-0010 L-0011 L-0012">

Telling the tagged person is one notification. Telling millions of people at once is a fan-out problem, and Kafka alone isn’t built for it. Kafka’s parallelism is limited by its number of partitions. [[L-0007]] Since Apache Kafka 4.2 (February 2026), share groups (“Kafka Queues”, KIP-932) are production-ready: several consumers in a share group can read the same partition and acknowledge each record on its own, so the consumers can outnumber the partitions <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2026/02/17/apache-kafka-4.2.0-release-announcement/">kafka.apache.org</Cite>.

Say Justin Bieber posts something. A notification has to reach 10 or 15 million followers, or however many there are: notifications to millions of users. [[L-0008]] For a fan-out that large, Kafka alone is painfully slow when you have a limited number of partitions, and adding a large number of partitions becomes a problem of its own. The cap is on delivery parallelism, so the per-user delivery work is handed to a queue that many workers can drain <Cite href="https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-932%3A+Queues+for+Kafka">KIP-932</Cite>. [[L-0009]]

The pattern: club a message stream (Kafka or Kinesis) with a message broker (SQS or RabbitMQ) to get a very high degree of fan-out. That is how notification systems are built at scale. [[L-0010]] AWS offers it as managed services: publish once to an SNS topic, and SNS pushes a copy to every subscribed SQS queue, where workers poll and process at their own pace <Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sns/latest/dg/sns-sqs-as-subscriber.html">docs.aws.amazon.com</Cite>. Drawing one message broker that broadcasts the message is not what happens at scale. [[L-0011]]

From practice: the instructor once had to notify about 50,000–60,000 users within five seconds, for an aggressive marketing push. Not millions, but in five seconds. The way to pull it off was to stitch a message stream to a message broker. [[L-0012]] Further watching: the instructor’s video on a full notification-system design for Instagram, the first on the instructor’s YouTube channel (sort by oldest). Most of it you can now design yourself; watch it for the reasoning behind this one fan-out decision. [[L-0006]]

</MarginNote>

<Section id="s4-4" no="4.4" title="Suggestions, activity and the feed" crumb="4.4 Suggestions and feed"
  dek="Tag suggestions put another team’s API in your request path. The activity page and the feed decide how tags must be stored, and who has to scale."
  meta="About 5 min · class 01:53–01:58">

You start typing a friend’s name, and the app already suggests who is in the photo.

One proposal is to suggest tags: “this photo looks like this person from your friends list”, the kind of thing Facebook does. That needs face-recognition and recommendation machine-learning systems. When a photo is uploaded, their inputs are fetched and shown to the user. [[L-0203]]

<SourceNote href="https://about.fb.com/news/2021/11/update-on-use-of-face-recognition/" k="L-0203">

Facebook did, until 2 November 2021. That day Meta shut down the Face Recognition system on Facebook and said it would delete more than a billion face templates; people can no longer turn on face recognition for suggested tagging. Products that do suggest tags this way still need face-recognition and recommendation ML services, as above.

</SourceNote>

Saying yes changes your design and pulls in another team. The front end has to change to display suggestions. More importantly, the suggestion service must be fast. If it isn’t, users sometimes see suggestions and sometimes don’t, which is a poor experience. That brings in the notion of an SLA. As soon as the user opens a photo and starts tagging, suggestions must appear, so you now have a **synchronous dependency** on the ML team’s API. That API needs a very strict SLA, and the team has to adhere to it. [[L-0204 L-0205]]

A synchronous dependency also needs a fallback plan: what if the API doesn’t respond in time? Do you block tagging, or let the user tag manually? It sounds straightforward, but it is still a product call. Some products might not allow tagging at all unless it comes from the ML team. [[L-0206 L-0207]] Fallbacks have their own risks. Amazon’s Builders’ Library argues against most of them: fallback code is hard to test, often has latent bugs, and can make an outage worse. It prefers making the primary path more robust and letting callers retry, and if a fallback is kept, running it continuously so it is always exercised <Cite href="https://builder.aws.com/content/3EuS9Sakq7L3VLQIF3qzfMfke1Y/avoiding-fallback-in-distributed-systems">builder.aws.com</Cite>.

<Figure id="fig-4e" no="Fig 4·e" k="L-0204 L-0205 L-0206 L-0207"
  caption="Suggestions put the ML team’s API on the request path: a synchronous call with a strict SLA. When it times out, the product decides between blocking tagging and letting people tag by hand. New figure.">
  <Desk><SyncSuggestions /></Desk>
  <Phone><SyncSuggestions layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Another proposal: put tags on the user’s activity page, where they can see what happened to them. Yes. That information is pushed to the profile service’s activity asynchronously, and the user themself is the one who sees it. [[L-0208]]

That brings out one very interesting part. The way you store “these people are tagged in this photo” must be indexed, because you also need to query it the other way round: in which photos is this user tagged? So the tags can’t just sit in an array inside the photo: there it is awkward to query the other way round at scale (the class called such an array unindexable; the note below gives the details). They go into a database that supports that query very efficiently. You can’t pick any random database here. [[L-0209]]

<SourceNote href="https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/arrays.html" k="L-0209">

Arrays can be indexed. MongoDB automatically makes an index on an array field a multikey index, with one entry per distinct value <Cite href="https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/core/indexes/index-types/index-multikey/">mongodb.com</Cite>, and PostgreSQL indexes arrays with GIN <Cite href="https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/gin.html">postgresql.org</Cite>. The point still holds for long tag lists: PostgreSQL’s own docs advise that if you search for array elements, a separate table with a row per element is easier to search and is likely to scale better.

</SourceNote>

A seemingly simple brief, and it already touches authorization, notifications, ML and storage. This is what typically happens in startups and mid-scale companies: senior management hasn’t thought the feature through, and only when you brainstorm and dive deep do you see how many systems it affects and interfaces with. [[L-0210]]

The feed is one more. If someone tags you in a photo, should your followers get that event in their feed? That is a product call. If yes, the feed team must gear up for the scale, because every tag pushes into feeds. The feed may also decide to show such posts to only a few users. [[L-0211]]

</Section>

<Section id="s4-5" no="4.5" title="Where exactly is the tag?" crumb="4.5 Tag position"
  dek="A tap on a face is a position on an image that will be resized everywhere. Store it as fractions, and as a box."
  meta="About 4 min · class 01:58–02:01">

On a 720×720 photo, the owner taps a friend’s head at pixel (320, 120). What goes into the database?

<PauseAndThink k="L-0212 L-0213 L-0215 L-0216"
  q="A low-end phone shows the same photo at 360×360. If you stored the tag as pixels (320, 120), does it still land on the face?"
  choices={[{ label: 'Yes: the phone scales the image, so the point scales with it' }, { label: 'No: store it relative to the image size', correct: true }]}
  right="Right: the numbers must scale with the image, so store them as fractions."
  wrong="Not quite: store it relative to the image size. The phone scales the image, not your stored numbers.">

Drawn at (320, 120) on a 360-pixel image, the tag sits near the right edge, far from the face, which is now at about (160, 60). Store `(320/720, 120/720)` ≈ `(0.44, 0.17)` and it lands right at any size with the same aspect ratio. Make it a box (LTRB, or x, y, w, h), since hovering shows the tag over a region. Images get resized everywhere, on different devices and by the on-demand transformations of [§3.4](#s3-4), and positions must survive that.

</PauseAndThink>

Not the pixels. Images get resized. On a low-end device the same photo may show at 360×360, and a tag drawn at x = 320 there lands near the right edge, nowhere near the face. [[L-0212]]

Store the relative position instead: 320/720 and 120/720, about (0.44, 0.17). As long as the aspect ratio is maintained, the tag lands right however the image is resized, on the smallest device or the largest. It is a small detail and a very important implementation decision. [[L-0213]] Facebook’s Graph API stores tag positions exactly this way: a tag’s x and y are given as a percentage offset from the left edge and from the top edge of the picture <Cite href="https://developers.facebook.com/docs/graph-api/reference/photo/tags/">developers.facebook.com</Cite>.

A tag also isn’t a single point. Your mouse doesn’t hover on one exact pixel to show who is tagged; it hovers over a region. So a tag is typically a **bounding box**, stored one of two ways: LTRB, the relative coordinates of the left-top and right-bottom corners, or x and y of one corner plus a relative width and height. Pick whichever the front end is more comfortable with. [[L-0215 L-0216]]

<Figure id="fig-4f" no="Fig 4·f" k="L-0212 L-0213 L-0214 L-0215 L-0216"
  caption="Stored as pixels, the tag lands off the head on a smaller copy. Stored as fractions, it lands on the head at any size. And a tag is really a box, written LTRB or x, y, w, h. Redrawn from the class board at 01:59–02:01.">
  <Desk><PixelsVsFractions /></Desk>
  <Phone><PixelsVsFractions layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

With relative boxes the front end can place every tag properly and show it on hover, even as the screen shrinks, grows or resizes. The board adds the other place images change size: the on-demand transformations of [§3.4](#s3-4), where the same original is served at any width. [[L-0217]] The board’s table for all this is one line: [[L-0214]]

```sql
posts_tags(post_id, user_id, location)
```

<Section id="s4-6" no="4.6" title="Everyone else hears about it through Kafka" crumb="4.6 Kafka for the rest"
  dek="Count the teams one sentence has touched. Each of them gets the tag the same way: one event on Kafka."
  meta="About 2 min · class 02:01–02:03">

Count the teams this one-line feature has touched.

Tagging interfaces with search, face detection, location, notification and role-based access control (RBAC: the authorization layer from [§4.2](#s4-2)), plus analytics, profile, feed and activity. [[L-0218]] One note on that label. In role-based access control, users are assigned roles and each role carries permissions <Cite href="https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/role-based-access-control">csrc.nist.gov</Cite>. “Who can tag me” is a per-user rule about other users, closer to an access control list. Google’s Zanzibar is a global system for storing and evaluating such ACLs, for services including Drive, Photos and YouTube <Cite href="https://research.google/pubs/zanzibar-googles-consistent-global-authorization-system/">research.google</Cite>.

Whatever the tagging service interfaces with gets its events through Kafka, no matter what. The service publishes one `PHOTO_TAG` event, and search, notification, analytics, profile, feed and activity each consume it. On the board, Kafka is the key component for extensibility. [[L-0219]]

None of this was in the brief. Senior management typically hasn’t thought about it, so think on their behalf and figure the details out, because the devil lies in the details. [[L-0220]]

And engineers can propose product ideas. You may not have product experience, but you can still pitch ideas and ask the critical questions a product team typically wouldn’t have thought of. [[L-0221]] Whenever you work with a product team, or on anything user-facing, think as an end user: put yourself in their shoes, then propose. That is how you create cross-team influence and a positive impact in the organization, and it leads to a good career. [[L-0222]]
