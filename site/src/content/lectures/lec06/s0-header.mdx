Open any profile page. A small round face. A friend tagged in a photo. A red **3** on the messages icon. Each looks like an afternoon’s work. In this lecture you build all three, and watch each one go wrong in a way the first sketch never shows.

The face is Gravatar: one URL, built from your email, that any site can put in an `<img>` tag to show your current photo. The one-line query that changes your photo hides four bugs, two of which can show a broken image on every site at once. The tag arrives as one sentence from a CXO, and it pulls in team after team before anyone draws a table. The red 3 ends with a pattern many companies run in production to keep their databases from falling over. [[L-0005 L-0055 L-0176 L-0223]]

Underneath all three sits one question: where does the truth live, and what do you keep ready next to it?

### Where you are

<LectureLink lec="lec09">Lecture 7</LectureLink> gave Instagram a photo pipeline: phones upload straight to S3 with a pre-signed URL, a CDN on its own hostname, `instacdn.net`, serves the photos and resizes them from a width in the URL, and the post stores only what can’t be derived, because anything derivable can be rebuilt when needed. It ended on a question: when the CDN resized a photo, who wrote that code? This lecture writes it. It leans on everything else too, and breaks the last rule on purpose.

Three older pieces come back too. <LectureLink lec="lec01">Lecture 2</LectureLink> called the database the most brittle part of a system and put Kafka between a write and everything that reacts to it. <LectureLink lec="lec02">Lecture 4</LectureLink> put every user’s WebSocket on edge servers, so the messaging side always knows who is connected. And <LectureLink lec="lec08">Lecture 5</LectureLink> used Redis’s `SET … NX` to take a lock; here the same command does a quieter job.

### What you’ll build

**Build 1, Gravatar (chapters 1–4): your face, on a million pages you never visit, changed in one click.** First, what a server actually does when an `<img>` tag asks for a picture, using a card nobody uploaded. Then the avatar service: two tables, the first query a request needs, a one-line update with four bugs, and a CDN in front that must know when your face changes and serve it at any size.

**Build 2, photo tags (chapter 5): one sentence from a CXO, and a schema that each new question breaks.** A different hat. The brief is one line, and the work is the questions: how many tags, who needs to look them up, where on a resized photo a tag sits, and which teams inherit the load.

**Build 3, the unread badge (chapters 6–8): a red 3 that screens ask for on every page load.** Define what the 3 counts, compute it the slow and always-correct way, make it cheap, then find out what cheap does to the database underneath.

### What you’ll be able to do

- Serve images, including ones that never existed until someone asked, from your own handler behind a CDN.
- Read a query the way the database will run it, and spot a full table scan before it ships.
- Find the bugs in a short write path: missing undo, a crash halfway, someone else’s id, two requests at once.
- Turn a one-line brief into the questions that change the design and the teams it pulls in.
- Choose between asking the source of truth and keeping a pre-computed answer, and protect the store that holds it.

### Before you start

You need SQL joins and indexes, a transaction, a CDN with an origin and a TTL, Kafka with consumers, and Redis as a key-value store (strings and sets). When a “Pause and think” box asks you something, commit to an answer before you open it. Every system here can be prototyped on your own laptop: the card in about an hour, Gravatar in an afternoon, the whole avatar service with its CDN and resizing in a couple of days. Building it is what makes the reasons stick. [[L-0051 L-0053 L-0061 L-0166]]
