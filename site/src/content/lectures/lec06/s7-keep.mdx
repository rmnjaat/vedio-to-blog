### Eleven lines to keep [[rail: Heuristics]]

The same few ideas kept coming back, whatever was on the board:

1. All that matters is the bytes you send. A handler can return an image that didn’t exist until someone asked ([§1.3](#s1-3)). [[L-0033]]
2. Drawing boxes is easy; the devil lies in the details. Write the exact query and see how it runs ([§2.3](#s2-3)). [[L-0085]]
3. Don’t store what’s derivable, unless not storing it slows things down. Then store it, as Gravatar stores the email’s hash ([§2.3](#s2-3)). [[L-0082]]
4. Check first, then write, so no change can leave the data inconsistent. And keep security at every step: careful in one place and lax in another doesn’t work ([§2.4](#s2-4)). [[L-0136 L-0172]]
5. The source of truth decides what gets served. S3 doesn’t know which photo is active; only the database does ([§3.2](#s3-2)). [[L-0140]]
6. Every requirement question should change the design, or positively influence it ([§4.3](#s4-3)). [[L-0191]]
7. Query the source of truth when you need strong consistency. For efficiency, pre-compute, or store the data in a form that is directly consumable ([§5.4](#s5-4)). [[L-0259]]
8. Crude is not incorrect. Counting over the messages table isn’t efficient, but it is correct ([§5.2](#s5-2)). [[L-0250]]
9. Protect the database from work that changes no state. It is the most brittle component: whichever cloud you pick, if the database is down, everything is down ([§6.1](#s6-1)). [[L-0288]]
10. Pick your battles: decide what you are trading off for what ([§6.3](#s6-3)). [[L-0297]]
11. Build it. Everything here is used in production somewhere and can be prototyped on your own laptop, without 50,000 servers ([§1.4](#s1-4)). [[L-0053]]

<MarginNote title="Getting good at query optimization" why="skip unless you want to get better at SQL" k="L-0312 L-0313 L-0314 L-0315 L-0316 L-0317 L-0318 L-0319 L-0320">

Every chapter here turned on a query. Getting good at reading them is a skill you can train, and it rests on depth in the database subject itself; that is where the instructor’s own foundation came from, in college. [[L-0312]] Understand how relational databases work and how data is structured, and go really deep into that. [[L-0313]]

The practical route: log into your production database console (MySQL, say, or your favourite database) and start firing `EXPLAIN` statements. Each one gives you the query plan. Look up every word in it: what it does, and how the plan was created. [[L-0316]] In MySQL, plain `EXPLAIN` shows how the optimizer would process the statement, while `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` actually runs it and reports real timings. On a production console, use `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` with care: heavy queries really execute, and data-changing statements really change data <Cite href="https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/explain.html">dev.mysql.com</Cite>.

Look the terms up in the database’s official documentation, not in blogs or videos. The documentation is dense, and it gives you the nitty-gritty details that videos and blogs typically overlook. [[L-0317]] Pick one query at a time, run `EXPLAIN`, look up the terms and iterate. Over three or four months you will be well-versed. [[L-0318]]

When you spot an optimization, try it on staging and see whether it improves anything. Or dump the data into another table and fire the exact same query with your change, and measure the improvement. Your company is a very good playground: the organization gets the improvement, and you learn. [[L-0320]] That loop is how the instructor built the skill: `EXPLAIN` on one real query at a time, every term looked up in the official docs, experiments on staging or a dumped copy. [[L-0319]]

Further study: the instructor’s members-only YouTube series on database internals, distilled from three college textbooks and courses. It focuses on what you use in day-to-day work rather than page structures, and doesn’t claim to cover everything. [[L-0314]] Planned parts, in order: phantom reads, SQL query optimization, index evaluation. [[L-0315]]
