It’s 11 p.m. You message a friend. The dot next to their name is green, so you wait for a reply. Nothing comes. The next morning: “Sorry, my phone died at 9.” For two hours the app told you they were there.

Every chat app has that dot. It looks like the easiest feature on the screen. The first version almost everyone writes is simple: when a user logs in, the app tells the server “I’m online”; when they log out, “I’m offline”. And it lies the moment a battery dies, because a dead phone never sends “offline”.

Fixing that one lie forces nearly every decision in the first half of this lecture: what to store, who deletes it, which database holds it, and why a tiny table can still bring its database to its knees. [[L-0021 L-0046]]

### The story

Picture your first month as the backend engineer at a small startup. Two tickets land on your desk.

**Part 1, ticket 1: make the green dot honest (chapters 1–5).** You start from the design everyone writes first, one true/false per user, and fix it one failure at a time: what the store must remember, who deletes it, which database holds it, and what happens to that database when a million users write at once.

**Part 2, ticket 2: the company’s blog (chapters 6–8).** The startup also runs a Medium-like blog, and its first month brings three complaints: a writer deletes the wrong post, the home page gets slower every week, and one post goes viral. Each forces a decision about what the database stores and where copies of it live.

The dot is tiny data with a brutal write rate. The blog is ordinary content that people care about. Between them they cover the two questions every design starts with: what must be stored, and what does it cost to read and write it.

### What you’ll be able to do

- Design a presence indicator that stays honest when devices die, and say why a logout call can never be the whole answer.
- Estimate storage and write rate in two lines of arithmetic, and size a connection pool against the database’s limit.
- Choose between two stores that both “work”, on the grounds that actually decide it.
- Decide soft or hard delete, column types and date formats from the queries that use them, and say what a cache really is.

### Before you start

You need to know what an API server, a database, an HTTP request and a TCP connection are. Nothing else is assumed: this is the first lecture. [[L-0016]]

Read it in order. Every section ends on a question, and the next section starts by answering it, so each fix leads to the problem it creates. Each chapter opens with a small map of the design so far, with the piece the last chapter added marked “new”. When a “Pause and think” box asks you something, commit to an answer before you open it.

Everything here runs on a laptop. After chapter 2 you can build the dot yourself: a tiny API, a local Redis and a script that sends heartbeats. Building it is what shows you where a design falters. [[L-0008 L-0009 L-0010]]
