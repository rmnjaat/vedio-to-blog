<Section id="s3" no="3" title="Two stores pass the test. Which one goes in the box?" crumb="3 Which store" meta="About 7 min">

Redis and DynamoDB both give you a key, a value and a TTL. Both are popular, fast and well documented. When two tools both meet the requirement, the feature list can’t decide between them. Something else has to, and it is usually something people forget to weigh. [[L-0071]]

<Figure id="fig-3-sofar" max={640} no="Fig 3·a" caption="The green dot so far. Marked new: the 30-second TTL on every write, so the store deletes silent users itself.">
<Desk><SoFarDot stage={2} /></Desk>
<Phone><SoFarDot stage={2} layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

This chapter takes the arguments one at a time, starting with the two everyone reaches for first: the data must persist, and the store must be fast.

</Section>

<Section id="s3-1" no="3.1" title="Do persistence or speed decide it?" crumb="3.1 First answers">

<PauseAndThink k="L-0072 L-0085"
  q="A teammate says: “DynamoDB, because our presence data has to persist.” Is persistence a good reason to pick a store for this table?"
  choices={[{ label: 'Yes: losing data is never acceptable' }, { label: 'No: ask what this data is worth first', correct: true }]}
  right="Right. Ask what an entry is worth before paying to keep it."
  wrong="Look at the data first: how long does an entry live?">

Every entry is deleted 30 seconds after the user’s last beat, on purpose. If the store crashed and lost every key, the next round of beats would rebuild the whole table within 10 seconds.

</PauseAndThink>

Persistence doesn’t even separate the two. Redis can write to disk as well: an append-only file (AOF) that logs every write, snapshots, or both. With the default setting the log is flushed once a second, so a crash can lose about a second of writes. Flushing on every write is safe but very slow <Cite href="https://redis.io/docs/latest/operate/oss_and_stack/management/persistence/">redis.io</Cite>. For heartbeats, a lost second is harmless. That is why Redis can be the *primary* store here, not just a cache in front of something else. Whether you need a database behind it depends on the guarantees your data needs, and this data needs almost none. [[L-0085 L-0086 L-0309 L-0310 L-0311]]

What about speed? Redis is faster. It serves from RAM over connections your server keeps open, in under a millisecond. DynamoDB is an HTTPS API that answers in single-digit milliseconds. But a few milliseconds is invisible next to a 30-second threshold. [[L-0093]] Speed will matter in one place, hearing about an expiry the moment it happens, and that comes up in section 3.4. [[L-0094]]

So the two obvious arguments don’t decide it. The ones that do are about people, money and next year, and they are the ones most often skipped. [[L-0100]] Start with people. Once this store is in production, who keeps it running?

</Section>

<Section id="s3-2" no="3.2" title="Who keeps it running?" crumb="3.2 Who runs it">

With DynamoDB, AWS does. It is **managed**: AWS keeps it available and scales it, and your team writes business logic. A self-hosted Redis is yours. Your engineers keep it up, watch it, scale it before a surge and react during one. Running any database yourself, Redis or MySQL or anything else, comes with its own pain: observability, profiling, upgrades. [[L-0073 L-0099 L-0313]]

So the first real question is about your team. A lean startup of five or ten engineers, with nobody who has run Redis in production, should go managed. You pay AWS, and it becomes your extended engineering team. Self-host only when you have both the expertise and the time. [[L-0139 L-0140]]

That seems to point at DynamoDB. It doesn’t, quite. Every major cloud also sells a managed Redis (AWS ElastiCache, for one), and your Redis code is the same everywhere. [[L-0087 L-0140]] How available a store is depends mostly on who operates it, though the product sets limits too. AWS commits to 99.99% availability for DynamoDB. Redis replicates asynchronously, so a failover can lose writes it already acknowledged, however good your team is. [[L-0304 L-0313]]

So “who runs it” narrows the choice to managed, but managed Redis and DynamoDB both qualify. What’s left is the one real difference between them. Redis is open source and runs anywhere. DynamoDB exists only on AWS. [[L-0098]] When would that matter?

</Section>

<Section id="s3-3" no="3.3" title="What if you have to leave your cloud?" crumb="3.3 Lock-in">

Then every line of code that talks to DynamoDB gets rewritten. Being tied to one cloud because a tool you depend on exists only there is called **vendor lock-in**. [[L-0074 L-0077 L-0078]]

Why would you ever leave? Pricing that grows out of hand. The rest of your infrastructure moving elsewhere. A better deal from another provider. A security incident that costs a provider your trust. A provider that keeps discontinuing services. People often fear lock-in more than it ends up costing them, but these reasons are real. [[L-0075 L-0076]]

The reason teams miss is **competition**. Amazon keeps entering new businesses. In 2020 it launched food delivery in Bengaluru. Any Indian food-delivery company running on AWS was now paying the company that had just become its competitor: its cloud bill funded the rival. Amazon closed that service at the end of 2022 <Cite href="https://techcrunch.com/2022/11/25/amazon-to-shut-down-food-delivery-business-in-india">techcrunch.com</Cite>, but the risk doesn’t go away. You may already be on a cloud when its owner walks into your market. [[L-0079]]

<Figure id="fig-3b" max={580} no="Fig 3·b" k="L-0079 L-0080" caption="Feeding your competitor: a food-delivery company pays a cloud vendor that runs its own food business. The bill funds the rival.">
<Desk><FeedingCompetitor /></Desk>
<Phone><FeedingCompetitor layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Walmart, Amazon’s rival in retail, stays off AWS for exactly this reason. It runs on a mix of Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud and its own private cloud. [[L-0080]] How much this matters grows with the bill. Paying a competitor $10 million a month matters; a young startup paying $10,000 doesn’t. [[L-0081 L-0082]] Netflix stays on AWS even though Amazon now runs a rival video service. Rewriting that much code would be enormous, and a customer that large may well have a deal that makes staying worthwhile, though its terms aren’t public. [[L-0083]] And a company that will never leave its cloud loses nothing to lock-in at all. [[L-0301]] Add the fact that far more engineers already know an open tool than one vendor’s product, and you see why many teams lean towards open source. [[L-0084 L-0302]]

Lock-in is about the day you might leave. The last argument is about staying: the store you pick now has to serve the feature as it grows. What will the dot need next year?

</Section>

<Section id="s3-4" no="3.4" title="What will the dot need next year?" crumb="3.4 Next year">

Today the dot needs a key and a timer. Next year it might need more. Redis isn’t only strings. It ships sorted sets, HyperLogLog for approximate counting, Bloom filters for fast “have I seen this?” checks, geospatial indexes and more. A later “friends online near you” or “how many people were online today” would have somewhere to live. DynamoDB covers some of that ground differently, with a change feed that triggers code you run beside it (Streams and Lambda). [[L-0088 L-0089 L-0090]]

Check what each store can do today, though, not its reputation. Databases keep borrowing each other’s features: MongoDB has ACID transactions now, and SQL databases shard. What a store “can’t do” is often a few years out of date. [[L-0091]]

One “next year” feature is close at hand. Suppose the product wants a friend’s dot to turn grey *the moment* they go offline, pushed to the screen instead of waiting for the next poll. Then your server needs to be **told** when a key expires.

<Figure id="fig-3c" max={640} no="Fig 3·c" k="L-0094 L-0097" caption="Hearing about an expiry. Left: the API server keeps a connection open to Redis, and Redis pushes “expired” on it when it deletes the key. Right: DynamoDB answers separate calls; an expired item lingers until it is deleted, and the stream reports it later.">
<Desk><HearingExpiry /></Desk>
<Phone><HearingExpiry layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Redis can do that, because your server holds a long-lived connection to it. When Redis deletes an expired key, it pushes an event on that connection. Two details matter. The event fires when Redis actually removes the key, which can be a little after the TTL runs out. And delivery is fire-and-forget, so treat it as a fast hint, not a guarantee <Cite href="https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/pubsub/keyspace-notifications/">redis.io</Cite>. DynamoDB has no open connection to push on. It reports deletions through its stream, later. [[L-0094 L-0097]]

```text
CONFIG SET notify-keyspace-events Ex   # expired-key events (off by default)
SUBSCRIBE __keyevent@0__:expired       # this connection now receives each expired key
```

That is every argument: persistence, speed, who runs it, lock-in and what comes next. Time to put them side by side and write one down.

</Section>

Two write-ups are both defensible here.

A lean team that wants nothing to operate and is already on AWS: **DynamoDB**, storing `expires_at` and checking it on every read.

A team that wants to hear about expiries the moment they happen, may need richer data structures later, or wants to stay free to change clouds: **Redis**, managed unless you have people who have run it before, with `SET … EX 30` on every beat. Redis as the primary store is fine for data this small.

Which one is right depends on your team, your cloud and what the feature will need next. What isn’t optional is writing the reason down. [[L-0311 L-0139 L-0140 L-0089 L-0097]] Next to the decision, record its limits and what a migration would take. When someone revisits the choice a year later, that record is what they will need. [[L-0317]]

<MarginNote title="Go deeper: shouldn’t you avoid database-specific features?" why="a common review objection" k="L-0314 L-0315 L-0316">

A reviewer may say: TTL isn’t universal, so don’t depend on it. But the design *needed* expiry, so it picked a store that has it. If you ever move, you move to one with similar features, or you rebuild the feature yourself (a cron job does it). The same goes for change streams: off DynamoDB, a change-data-capture tool such as Debezium reads MySQL’s binlog instead. Draw a line: abstract over everything and you end up writing your own database.

</MarginNote>

The store is chosen, and the design looks finished. But every estimate so far has counted rows. None has counted how often those rows are written.

</Section>
