<Section id="s5-2" no="5.2" title="Delete, or mark deleted?" crumb="5.2 Soft delete"
  dek="The database gives you DELETE. Many tables never use it. Three reasons why."
  meta="About 6 min · class 01:41–01:48">

Why add an `is_deleted` column when the database gives you `DELETE`? That column is a **soft delete**. When the user deletes a blog, you don’t remove the row; you mark `is_deleted` as true. Many tables have such a column, probably some in your own organisation. Soft deletes are extremely important, including for the database’s performance (that reason comes in 5.3). [[L-0173]]

A **hard delete** is what you get when you fire a `DELETE` query on the database. The data is gone, and you cannot recover it again. [[L-0174]]

<PauseAndThink k="L-0175 L-0176 L-0177 L-0179 L-0180 L-0183"
  q="A writer deletes a blog post. Your schema has an <code>is_deleted</code> column. Is there a good reason to set it to true instead of running <code>DELETE</code>?"
  choices={[{ label: 'No: deleted means gone' }, { label: 'Yes, more than one', correct: true }]}
  right="Right. There are three, and one involved a police station."
  wrong="For some tables, yes. For user content there are at least three reasons to wait.">

Three: **recoverability**, **archival** and **audit**, each explained below. When none applies, or a unique email must be freed for a re-signup ([§5.4](#s5-4)), hard delete.

</PauseAndThink>

**Reason 1: recoverability.** To let a user recover a deleted blog, you need a soft delete. [[L-0175]] Google Drive works this way: a deleted file sits in the trash for 30 days, recoverable at any point. [[L-0176]]

**Reason 2: archival.** Content a user put effort into is rich data. It can, for example, train machine-learning models (“build another ChatGPT”). Hard delete it and that data is gone. Where users have a legal right to erasure, keeping deleted content for a new purpose like model training needs its own legal basis or proper anonymisation <Cite href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/">gdpr-info.eu</Cite>. [[L-0177]] The mechanics: soft delete the row, then a job moves soft-deleted rows into S3 or another blob storage, for machine-learning training and other uses. [[L-0178]]

**Reason 3: audit.** Deleted data may be needed later as evidence. [[L-0179]] A consumer platform has to provide such information to the authorities, and police and government don’t want evidence of harassment to disappear. [[L-0181]]

<MarginNote title="From practice: the police station" why="skip unless you like war stories" k="L-0180 L-0182">

At a startup, one user harassed another online with a filthy comment. The victim filed a police complaint, the police asked the platform for the user’s details, and the harasser promptly deleted the comment. With a hard delete, the evidence would have been gone. [[L-0180]] An engineer from the platform ended up at the police station, giving justifications and handing over printed copies of all the traces: something no engineer expects to do. [[L-0182]]

</MarginNote>

So when you design a table, ask three questions of its entries: recoverability? archival? audit? If yes, soft delete; otherwise, hard delete. [[L-0183]]

<RuleBox rule="Ask of every table: does it need ==recoverability, archival or audit==? If yes, soft delete." k="L-0183">

The board’s line for the blog: when the user invokes *delete blog*, instead of `DELETE` we `UPDATE`. If none of the three applies, a hard delete is fine.

<Desk><ThreeQuestions /></Desk>
<Phone><ThreeQuestions layout="phone" /></Phone>

</RuleBox>

The habit behind the rule: design from a holistic view, not from the narrow vision of just building things. [[L-0184]] In general, tables that store user-generated information soft delete. [[L-0188]]

</Section>

<Section id="s5-3" no="5.3" title="The B+ tree argument" crumb="5.3 B+ tree"
  dek="A fourth reason: soft deletes are easy on the engine. The argument, and what engines actually do."
  meta="About 4 min · class 01:46–01:48, Q&A 02:06–02:08">

A fourth reason sits under the three: *easy on the database engine (no tree re-balancing)*. Most storage engines store data as a **B+ tree**. When you hard delete a row, its entry is removed from a B+ tree node, the tree becomes imbalanced, and the engine has to rebalance it, spending CPU cycles on rebalancing instead of on requests. [[L-0185]]

<SourceNote href="https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/index-page-merge-threshold.html" site="dev.mysql.com" k="L-0185">

Engines defer the physical work of a delete: InnoDB removes the row and its index records later, in **purge**, and in PostgreSQL a `DELETE` “does not immediately remove the old version” of a row; `VACUUM` reclaims it <Cite href="https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/innodb-purge-configuration.html">dev.mysql.com</Cite> <Cite href="https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/routine-vacuuming.html">postgresql.org</Cite>. InnoDB merges an index page with a neighbour only when its fill drops below `MERGE_THRESHOLD`, 50% by default. So a hard delete does not trigger a rebalance each time. Choose soft delete for recovery, archival and audit, not as a B+ tree optimisation.

</SourceNote>

With a soft delete, the entry is only updated and still exists, so the node doesn’t need rebalancing and the database carries less load. [[L-0186]] It is still a write, though. In InnoDB an update to an indexed column is a delete-mark plus an insert in that index, and PostgreSQL writes a new version of the row, so the saving over a hard delete is smaller than “no rebalancing” suggests <Cite href="https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/routine-vacuuming.html">postgresql.org</Cite>. [[L-0186]]

<Figure id="fig-5c" max={900} no="Fig 5·c" k="L-0185 L-0186"
  caption="Hard delete vs soft delete in the tree. The first two panels are the argument as taught. More precisely (the note above): engines defer deletes and merge pages by fill, not per delete.">
  <Desk><TreeDelete /></Desk>
  <Phone><TreeDelete layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Whichever way your engine goes, the lesson that survives: know your database’s internals, how it structures data, to judge whether a soft delete helps. [[L-0187]]

Doesn’t the benefit vanish once the rows are eventually hard deleted (Google Drive after 30 days)? Not quite. Hard deleting each row on its own can, in the worst case, trigger a rebalance per delete; soft-deleted rows can be accumulated and hard deleted in one batch. [[L-0228]] Clubbing the deletes clubs the rebalancing: once or a few times, not on every pointed delete. [[L-0229]] Delete 100 rows at a time and commit, and you get one rebalance per 100 rows, a factor of 100 fewer. [[L-0230]]

Batching does pay, for a slightly different reason: page merges depend on how full a page is, so what batching mainly saves is transaction, locking and log overhead per row. MySQL advises big purges as a `DELETE … LIMIT row_count` repeated until fewer rows are affected, so no single delete runs too long <Cite href="https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/delete.html">dev.mysql.com</Cite>. [[L-0228 L-0229 L-0230]]

</Section>

<Section id="s5-4" no="5.4" title="The soft-delete lifecycle" crumb="5.4 Lifecycle"
  dek="Soft-deleted rows pile up. Archive them, hard delete them later, and spread the deletes out."
  meta="About 6 min · Q&A 02:22–02:46">

Soft-deleted rows grow the database, so they are hard deleted periodically, say once a day. [[L-0263]] Or they are moved somewhere else; that is archival. [[L-0264]] When a soft-deleted item can move on to a hard delete is very subjective: a 30-day trash, a day, or a legal deadline (5.5). [[L-0265]]

Soft delete and archival are not the same thing. A soft delete *gives you time* to archive: after a day, say, collect the soft-deleted rows, put them elsewhere, then hard delete them. A hard delete leaves no time; imagine copying each row to S3 as you delete it. [[L-0308]]

<Figure id="fig-5d" max={880} no="Fig 5·d" k="L-0178 L-0263 L-0264 L-0308 L-0283 L-0296"
  caption="The life of a deleted blog: marked, recoverable for a while, archived by a background job, then hard deleted in small batches. The side branch is the one case where a soft delete gets in the way.">
  <Desk><DeletedLife /></Desk>
  <Phone><DeletedLife layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

**Hard deleting under traffic.** A job must hard delete 100,000 soft-deleted rows while traffic is high. Don’t do it in one shot. Split the work across the day, like garbage collection: 10,000 rows every hour, or about 1,000 at a time, at a steady pace, so the main service barely notices. [[L-0283 L-0230]]

<AnimationClip clip="l04-staggered-delete" no="Clip 5·1" k="L-0263 L-0282 L-0283 L-0295 L-0297 L-0298"
  lead="100,000 soft-deleted rows, peak traffic. One DELETE, or many small ones?"
  caption="Stagger the hard delete, whether a cron job or the database runs it. One huge delete puts its whole load on the database at once; the service does not go down (the note below). The request queue and its slowdown are illustrative." />

<MarginNote title="From practice: the interview question" why="skip unless you interview" k="L-0282 L-0284 L-0285">

An interviewer once pushed a candidate: a background job hard deletes the soft-marked entries, but the service must be highly available, so how do you handle the downtime while it deletes? A cron job has nothing to do with availability. There is no reason the main service should go down because of a delete; stagger the deletes at a linear pace. [[L-0282]]

The real worry was locking the table entries while deleting, and that is about **strong consistency**, not availability: you lock and delete, rather than deleting directly, so that nobody reads an entry that is already deleted. [[L-0284]] <ForwardChip when="Lecture 3" k="L-0285">strong consistency, in the databases session</ForwardChip>

</MarginNote>

The hourly job isn’t free either: it takes one or two connections from the pool ([§4.3](#s4-3)), while not deleting lets the data grow. You gain some, you lose some; you never win it all. [[L-0295]]

A retention policy set in the database itself still needs someone to delete: a database thread runs the cron job for you; you have offloaded it. [[L-0297]] The timing is the database’s, too: MongoDB’s TTL indexes are enforced by a background task every 60 seconds, so under load expired documents can outlive their expiry by a minute or more <Cite href="https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/core/index-ttl/">mongodb.com</Cite>. [[L-0297]]

For deletes at regular intervals, what always works for database performance: offload it to the database, and stagger it. It is the same instinct as the TTL in [§2.8](#s2-8). [[L-0298]]

**One case where soft delete gets in the way.** `users.email` has a uniqueness index; a user deletes their account and signs up again with the same email. The soft-deleted row still holds that email and blocks the signup, so here the row has to be hard deleted. [[L-0296]] Another way out is a partial unique index, enforcing uniqueness only for rows not deleted <Cite href="https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/indexes-partial.html">postgresql.org</Cite>. [[L-0296]]

```sql
-- PostgreSQL: unique email,
-- live rows only
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX users_email_live
  ON users(email)
  WHERE NOT is_deleted;

-- MySQL: hard delete in batches;
-- repeat until < 1000 rows go
DELETE FROM blogs
  WHERE is_deleted = 1
  LIMIT 1000;
```

</Section>

<Section id="s5-5" no="5.5" title="The right to be forgotten" crumb="5.5 Right to be forgotten"
  dek="When a user asks for everything to go, the law sets a deadline. What really gets deleted, and what the rules say."
  meta="About 4 min · Q&A 02:22–02:26">

A Facebook user files a ticket: delete everything you have on me.

Under GDPR’s **right to be forgotten**, Facebook then has to hard delete everything that user ever created, within one month. [[L-0266]] The right is not absolute: GDPR lets an organisation keep data it needs for a legal obligation, for legal claims, for freedom of expression, for public-interest archiving or research, and a few other grounds <Cite href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/">gdpr-info.eu</Cite>. [[L-0266]]

It doesn’t have to be immediate. If that user had harassed someone, the one-to-two-month window gives the company breathing space. [[L-0267]]

<SourceNote href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-12-gdpr/" site="gdpr-info.eu" k="L-0267">

GDPR gives one month to act on an erasure request, extendable by two further months for complex or numerous requests; the person must be told within the first month. Keeping data for a case like harassment rests on a specific exception (legal claims, Art. 17(3)), not on the deadline <Cite href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/">gdpr-info.eu</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

As taught, PII is deleted immediately, but logs have everything, so there are hacks to recover data. [[L-0268]]

<SourceNote href="https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-erasure/" site="ico.org.uk" k="L-0268">

Live personal data can be erased at once. Copies in logs and backups must be put “beyond use” (not restored or used) and overwritten on their normal schedule, not kept so the data can be recovered <Cite href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/">gdpr-info.eu</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

The example: Atlassian had a mega outage in which data was deleted and took 14 days to recover it. If they could recover it, no company hard deletes all the data ever created; they hard delete, for compliance, from specific tracked places, while logs and snapshots still hold everything. [[L-0269]] The outage was real, but not an erasure request: in April 2022 a deletion script was given the wrong IDs and deleted 883 sites for 775 customers, which Atlassian restored from its backups over up to 14 days <Cite href="https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage">atlassian.com</Cite>. Restoring accidental deletions is what backups are for. [[L-0269]]

<Figure id="fig-5e" max={640} no="Fig 5·e" k="L-0266 L-0268 L-0270"
  caption="Where an erased user’s data still lives. As taught, nobody rewrites old snapshots; under the rules, those copies must stay beyond use and age out on schedule.">
  <Desk><ErasedData /></Desk>
  <Phone><ErasedData layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Snapshots make the same point. If you take daily snapshots and a user exercises the right to be forgotten, nobody goes back and updates the historical snapshots. The data is still there in the old snapshots, but the transactional system holds no trace of it. [[L-0270]] Regulator guidance accepts this if that backup data is put beyond use, overwritten on the normal schedule, and the person is told what happens to it <Cite href="https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-erasure/">ico.org.uk</Cite>. [[L-0270]]

In the lecture’s view these are “compliance hacks”, with the legal team helping you navigate the compliance part to maximise what you can. [[L-0271]] The legitimate ones, backups kept beyond use and data held under an Art. 17(3) exception, are recognised parts of the rules, written into regulator guidance, not loopholes. [[L-0271]]

<MarginNote title="From practice: making an org GDPR compliant" why="skip unless you’ll build it" k="L-0272">

Making an entire organisation GDPR compliant, the infrastructure-level pieces included, took one engineering effort two months, and that work is where these details come from. [[L-0272]]

</MarginNote>

GDPR, in one line: the regulation behind the right to be forgotten. Ask Instagram to delete everything about you, as if you had never existed on it, and it has to hard delete all your data; GDPR enforces that on every single organisation. [[L-0273]]

<SourceNote href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-3-gdpr/" site="gdpr-info.eu" k="L-0273">

GDPR applies to organisations established in the EU, and to others that offer goods or services to, or monitor, people in the EU. Within that scope, users can demand erasure on specified grounds, subject to exceptions <Cite href="https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/">gdpr-info.eu</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

So much for whether a row stays. The next question is what goes inside it.
