<Section id="s9" no="9" title="The whole hashtag design" crumb="9 The whole design"
  dek="The top 100 arrives from another team without a single synchronous call, the page is served from a CDN, and the read path / write path method checks every hop."
  meta="About 22 min · class 02:18–02:35, Q&A 02:36–02:37, 02:41–02:42, 02:46–02:50">

Data science has just recomputed the top 100 for `#sunset`. Your hashtag page must show it, and your page must never go down because their service did.

The document from [7.4](#s7-4) has three parts: the tag, the count that [chapter 8](#s8) keeps up to date, and the top 100. This chapter fills in the last one, draws the whole service on one page, and then checks it with a method you can run on any design.

</Section>

<Section id="s9-1" no="9.1" title="The top 100 arrives on Kafka" crumb="9.1 The top 100"
  dek="Another team computes the ranking. Your page needs a copy of it, delivered without a single synchronous call."
  meta="About 8 min · class 02:19–02:23, Q&A 02:41–02:43, 02:46–02:50">

How would this top 100 get into your document? It is tempting to start by defining what “top” means and sorting posts by some metric. Don’t: that part is a black box. The data science team decides what popular means and computes it, and how they do it is not your concern. You own this page, and only this page. [[L-0259]]

The next temptation is to call their service whenever the page needs the top photos, since it already knows them. You can’t. The page renders from one read of your own database ([7.4](#s7-4)), so the data has to be stored here. Assume their service notifies you whenever it generates the top photos for a hashtag. The real question is how they tell you there is a new top 100 for `#sunset`. Will they give you a call? [[L-0260]]

<PauseAndThink k="L-0260 L-0264 L-0305"
  q="Data science recomputes the top 100 for <code>#sunset</code> every so often. Should your hashtag page call their service when it renders, or get the result some other way?"
  choices={[{ label: 'Call their API on each render: it’s always fresh' }, { label: 'Consume their updates from Kafka and store them', correct: true }]}
  right="Right. Copy their output into your own store; don’t borrow their uptime."
  wrong="Not quite. Fresh on every render means down whenever they are, and slower every time.">

Consume from Kafka and store a copy. A synchronous call on every render makes your page’s uptime and latency depend on their service, and the page must render in one read. Webhooks into your API are better, but they put retries and your downtime on their plate: if your API is down, the update is lost. With their Kafka, a small process reads at its own pace and replaces the top-100 array in your document. Anything that doesn’t need to be synchronous shouldn’t be.

</PauseAndThink>

One idea raised in class: a separate service that listens for their notifications through a messaging queue and updates the store the moment a new batch for `#sunset` arrives, with the worry that it means paying for a service that runs all the time. The idea is right and the worry is misplaced. They give you the data in Kafka, you read from it and you update your store. Simple. [[L-0261]] Calling every consumer a “service” is a common mistake, and it makes a small job sound heavyweight. This is a process: a simple set of servers whose only job is to consume and update. [[L-0262]]

So the flow is this. Whenever the data science team’s popularity service generates the top photos for a hashtag, it pushes an event onto Kafka, their Kafka, which is the best place for such notifications. Your workers consume that event, take the top 100 post IDs out of it and update them in the hashtag database. They may keep the result in their own database as well; you copy it into yours, structured your own way, because you want to guarantee a very fast response time. This stream is not high throughput, so a few workers are plenty. [[L-0263]]

What does “update” mean for the array? It depends on what data science sends, but most likely they give you a complete top 100 every time, and you simply replace the entire array in the document. Nothing is read back and re-sorted. [[L-0309]]

Why go to this trouble instead of calling their service on each read? Because a synchronous dependency makes the uptime of your service depend on the uptime of theirs. Wherever you can, model things asynchronously. [[L-0264]]

Here you see Kafka as the glue that joins several services together, and that ability is why Kafka became so widely famous. [[L-0265]] In the lecture’s telling, the CQRS pattern you may have heard of exists because of this ability, and it makes things neat. [[L-0266]]

<SourceNote href="https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CQRS.html" site="martinfowler.com" k="L-0266">

CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) means using a different model to update information than the one you read it from. It predates Kafka: Martin Fowler credits Greg Young with it. Building a separate, read-optimised store from events, as the hashtag service does, is in its spirit, and Kafka is a common way to connect the two models, not their cause. Fowler also warns that for most systems CQRS adds risky complexity.

</SourceNote>

The same glue works in the other direction. Suppose data science needs hashtag-level data for its popularity ranking. They don’t have to rebuild the extraction you already did: they can consume `POST_HASHTAG`, the `<hashtag, post>` topic from [8.3](#s8-3), themselves. Today the counting servers are its only consumer; tomorrow data science might start reading it for their own crunching, if they want. [[L-0299]]

**Webhooks instead?** Another proposal: top-100 updates are rare, so let the popularity service call your hashtag API directly through webhooks, with no Kafka in between. That is a good approach, but weigh it. The data science team would have to implement and manage the calls into your API, and they will say that isn’t their job: writing to Kafka and being done is what they would prefer. And if your hashtag API happens to be down when they call, the update is lost, and they won’t reprocess it for you. [[L-0305]] Everything in computer science is a trade-off; what you need to know is what you are trading for what. [[L-0306]]

The deciding rule is to decouple as much as you can. Anything that doesn’t need to happen synchronously, in real time, shouldn’t be done synchronously. A popularity update doesn’t demand synchronicity, which makes it a very nice use case for asynchronous consumption. [[L-0307]] <ForwardChip label="from" when="Lecture 2" lec="lec01" sec="s3-1" k="L-0307">whatever doesn’t need real time shouldn’t be real time</ForwardChip>

</Section>

<Section id="s9-2" no="9.2" title="The finished architecture" crumb="9.2 The architecture"
  dek="Three paths into one partitioned database: posts counted, the top 100 copied in, and readers served from a CDN."
  meta="About 5 min · class 02:23–02:27, 02:34–02:35">

Here is the whole hashtag service on one page. On the write side, the post service puts each post on Kafka, the hashtag extractor turns it into `<hashtag, post>` tuples on `POST_HASHTAG`, and the counting servers batch them into a partitioned database. Beside it, the popularity service writes to its own Kafka, and a few workers copy each new top 100 into the same database. On the read side, a user’s `GET /hashtag/<tag>` goes to a CDN, the CDN to the hashtag API, and the API to the partitioned database, with a cache. [[L-0268]]

The read side is the simple one. Hashtag API workers serve a normal GET request: on each request they read from the database the entire document built for the page ([7.4](#s7-4)) and send it to the user. [[L-0271]] The access is key value: for a key, the hashtag, get the value, its details. Any partitioned store that does that well will serve, such as MySQL, MongoDB or DynamoDB. [[L-0274]]

Most of that document doesn’t change often, so put the endpoint behind a CDN. The page then renders immediately: users are served from the CDN instead of waiting relatively longer for a database read, and the CDN takes care of geographic distribution. [[L-0272]] A CDN caches JSON API responses just as it caches images. It caches whatever the origin sends and doesn’t care whether that is an image, JSON or text. [[L-0273]] That needs the origin’s cooperation, though: many CDNs don’t cache JSON by default (Cloudflare caches by file extension, and skips responses marked `private`, `no-store` or `no-cache` or carrying a `Set-Cookie`), so the hashtag API should send `Cache-Control` with an `s-maxage`, or you add a cache rule, as in [2.3](#s2-3). <Cite href="https://developers.cloudflare.com/cache/concepts/default-cache-behavior/">developers.cloudflare.com</Cite> <ForwardChip label="from" when="Lecture 2" lec="lec01" sec="s1-5" k="L-0273">the CDN as a cache level</ForwardChip>

CDN or cache? Put either in front of the API and pick your favourite; with a CDN you don’t need the cache. The lecture’s choice is the CDN, any day, because the response is barely changing. [[L-0275]]

Then keep it fresh. Configure a TTL on the CDN, and/or invalidate the cached response explicitly whenever the top 100 or the count changes. Once the key expires, the next request goes to the API, which loads the document from the database; the result is cached and served again. [[L-0276]] To spare the API a burst of requests at the moment a popular tag’s copy expires, the origin can add `stale-while-revalidate=M` to its `Cache-Control` header (next to `max-age=N`): the CDN keeps serving the stale copy for up to M seconds while it fetches a fresh one in the background. <Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/Expiration.html">docs.aws.amazon.com</Cite>

```http
GET /hashtag/sunset

200 OK
Content-Type: application/json
Cache-Control: s-maxage=60
Cache-Control: stale-while-revalidate=30

{ "tag": "sunset",
  "total_photos": …,
  "top_100": [ …100 post IDs… ] }
```

Look at the figure once more and count the Kafkas. The post topic feeds every other service (search, notification, feed). It is re-partitioned by hashtag to make the counting writes efficient. And the data science team talks to you, the backend engineers, through Kafka. That is Kafka as the glue: everyone is decoupled, and everyone works at their own pace. [[L-0289]]

</Section>

<Section id="s9-3" no="9.3" title="Read path, write path" crumb="9.3 Read and write paths"
  dek="A method you can run on any design: split it into the paths that write and the paths that read, then push each hop until it breaks."
  meta="About 9 min · class 02:27–02:35, Q&A 02:36–02:37">

This is how almost every senior engineer works. To build any system well, understand what its read paths and write paths are, and tackle one at a time. [[L-0277]] Pick any system so far, literally any: some paths write to the database and some paths read from it. Whenever you design or architect a system, pick one path and check whether it is heavily optimized. [[L-0278]]

Start with the simplest version of the hashtag diagram. Post service → Kafka → hashtag workers → database is the write path, where you write to the database. The hashtag API reading from the database and serving it is the read path. As the board put it, the hashtag API servers won’t count; the worker nodes batch and update. [[L-0279]]

<Figure id="fig-9b" no="Fig 9·b" k="L-0279 L-0274 L-0277 L-0280 L-0291 L-0293"
  caption="The two paths. The write path (dashed: it crosses async hand-offs) runs post service → Kafka → hashtag workers → partitioned database. The read path runs user → hashtag API → partitioned database or cache, a key-value lookup. Blue arrows are data. Redrawn from the class board at 02:28.">
  <Desk><TwoPaths /></Desk>
  <Phone><TwoPaths layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Optimize the read path first; it is the easy part. You typically add a cache and/or a CDN, and check that you are doing enough for the read path to have the bare minimum latency. The board’s list: a centralized cache, a hashtag API that does the bare minimum work, and a database that can handle the reads. [[L-0280]]

Once the read path is done, walk the write path, step by step, and at each step ask whether it does the bare minimum. Something as simple as post service → Kafka must be a single API call. With two calls you would need a distributed transaction, and to ensure consistency, and whatnot. Assume you need low latency here too. [[L-0281]]

From Kafka to the hashtag workers: the number of workers you need to process from Kafka equals the number of partitions of the topic. [[L-0282]] More precisely, that is the most that can usefully work in parallel: within a consumer group each partition is assigned to exactly one consumer, so consumers beyond the partition count get no partitions. <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/43/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/consumer/KafkaConsumer.html">kafka.apache.org</Cite> <ForwardChip label="from" when="Lecture 2" lec="lec01" sec="s3-6" k="L-0282">topics, partitions, consumer groups</ForwardChip>

Into the database: atomic updates, partial updates. Is the database provisioned enough? Are you consuming at a decent enough pace? At every single step of the write, ask whether you are doing the bare minimum. (The board’s note for this path: ingestion in Kafka; requirements from storage? partial updates.) [[L-0283]]

Looking at the read path and the write path separately, and tackling each one, gives you a very structured way to build robust systems. And when you pick one path, think of the implementation: think of what can go wrong. [[L-0284]]

<RuleBox rule="Understand your read paths and your write paths, and ==tackle one at a time==." k="L-0277 L-0285">

At every hop, think of the implementation and blow it out of proportion: what if this gets a huge load? Fix it, re-architect, and move to the next hop. Almost every senior engineer works this way.

<Phone><OnePathSketch layout="phone" /></Phone>

</RuleBox>

When you optimize any path, think of the implementation at every single step, because that is where most things get neglected. Make every component blow out of proportion (what if it gets a huge load?), fix it, and re-architect, one path at a time. That is all “read path / write path optimization” means. [[L-0285]]

Blow up the read path first. A user calls the hashtag API, which just talks to the database. What if that one database gets high spikes? Decide whether to partition it, and whether you need replicas. Add a cache to take load off the database. If that is not enough, add a CDN in front of it. [[L-0286]]

Now the write path, thought through as an implementation. Take a post with eight hashtags. The naive way extracts the eight hashtags from the caption and makes eight writes to the database. Is that good enough? Not really: it is a lot of micro writes, which is why you want batching. [[L-0287]] To batch, everything for one hashtag must reach one server. So you extract the hashtags and re-ingest them into another Kafka, partitioned by hashtag; the server that owns a hashtag batches its tuples and then writes to the database. That is exactly how [8.3](#s8-3) arrived at its adapter. [[L-0288]]

The board’s key takeaways name two more habits from this design: the adapter pattern, and effective batching and counting. The naive approach does `count++` for every event, and that is not how a system works. At every step, find that pattern and fix it. [[L-0290]]

Read path / write path optimization is almost the standard way to think about systems: work out what the read paths are and what the write paths are, then tackle one at a time, because standard practice is to scale the reads separately. [[L-0291]]

Does that mean the read path and the write path must live in separate services? No. In this design the hashtag web server and the asynchronous counting workers do run separately, but that is because they have different responsibilities: one is a web server, the others are asynchronous workers. They were treated as separate concerns; the read/write split itself was not the reason. [[L-0293]]
