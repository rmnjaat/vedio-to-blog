Your servers are in Singapore. Someone in the US opens a 10 MB photo on Instagram. Then they tap `#sunset` and expect a page at once: “1.2m photos” and the top 100 of them. Instagram reached 14 million users in its first year with three engineers <Cite href="https://highscalability.com/instagram-architecture-14-million-users-terabytes-of-photos/">highscalability.com</Cite>, so whatever serves those photos and that page can’t need an army to run. [[L-0007 L-0027 L-0211]]

Draw it on a whiteboard and you get two boxes behind the app: API servers and a database. Photos go in, photos come out, a count goes up. It looks like a simple problem, and it is one. Yet go one level deeper, into what actually happens to the bytes and the numbers, and nearly every assumption those two boxes made gets broken. [[L-0002 L-0152]]

### Where you are

<LectureLink lec="lec01">Lecture 2</LectureLink> put a CDN between the reader and your servers and cached responses there, and it sent the blog’s publish events through Kafka: one topic split into partitions by `user_id`, read by consumer groups that commit how far they got. <LectureLink lec="lec02">Lecture 4</LectureLink> pushed Slack messages through Redis Pub/Sub, and watched a push vanish when a server wasn’t listening at that moment. <LectureLink lec="lec03">Lecture 6</LectureLink> gave every Instagram photo a 64-bit ID, made inside the database, that sorts by time.

All of that comes back here. Lecture 2 used the CDN as a cache; this lecture opens it just enough to use it well, as a black box you configure rather than build. [[L-0003 L-0017]]

### What you’ll build

**Build 1, Instagram’s photos (chapters 1–5).** A photo is big: a few megabytes, sometimes tens. Your target is 5 million uploads a day, and a photo posted in one country is opened in many others. The problem is the bytes: how they reach every reader fast, how they get in without flooding your API servers, what you store about them, and who is allowed to see them. [[L-0025 L-0063 L-0061]]

**Build 2, the hashtag page (chapters 6–9).** Every hashtag has a page that must open in one request, for millions of hashtags, with a count that every new post can change. The bytes are small now. The problem is the work: a write for every tag of every post, a top 100 that another team computes, and a page that must render instantly anyway. Chapter 5 ends with the hand-off between the two. [[L-0211 L-0212 L-0213]]

### What you’ll be able to do

- Put a CDN in front of any URL, and get photos in and out at Instagram’s volume without your API servers paying for it.
- Decide what a table stores and what it computes, and say what “private” means for a photo on a CDN.
- Keep a count that millions of posts change, on a stream other teams already depend on.
- Check a design path by path, and run a Kafka pipeline through outages, restarts and growth.

### Before you start

You need HTTP requests and responses, DNS, what an object store like S3 is, and Kafka topics, partitions and consumer groups from <LectureLink lec="lec01">Lecture 2</LectureLink>.

Each chapter opens on the design as it stands, with the piece the last chapter added marked. When a “Pause and think” box asks you something, commit to an answer before you open it. Most of the ideas in this lecture come from first answering wrong and finding out why.
