Instagram is one year old. It has grown to fourteen million users with three engineers, and every founder starting a company wants to know one thing: what are they running?

This short chapter answers that question, and then asks a better one: should you copy it?

<Section id="s1-1" no="1.1" title="What Instagram ran in year one" crumb="1.1 Year-one stack"
  dek="A web framework, a task queue, a cache and two kinds of database, all rented from AWS."
  meta="About 3 min · class 00:02–00:04">

Instagram saw a huge number of users in its first year: fourteen million, with three engineers. That was insane at the time. (The board doesn’t say what the fourteen million counts, only “1 year, 14 million, 3 engineers”.) [[L-0007]]

Its architecture at the end of that year was Django in the backend; Celery as the asynchronous processor, with RabbitMQ or Redis passing the messages for that distributed, asynchronous processing; Redis as a cache; a transactional database and a non-relational one. All of it was hosted on AWS: by then AWS had pretty decent offerings, and Instagram leveraged them. [[L-0009]]

The published account of that year fills in the names <Cite href="https://highscalability.com/instagram-architecture-14-million-users-terabytes-of-photos/">highscalability.com</Cite>: Django (on Gunicorn) behind nginx on EC2, PostgreSQL as the main database, Redis for feeds and sessions, memcached as the cache, and the photos on S3 behind CloudFront. Background tasks first ran on Gearman; as task volume grew to thousands per second, Instagram replaced it with Celery workers and a RabbitMQ broker <Cite href="https://pycon-archive.python.org/2013/schedule/presentation/106">PyCon 2013</Cite>. [[L-0009]]

Look at the picture again and every building block of the course’s first week is in it: caching, the database, communication, asynchronous processing, scaling. Every single thing. <ForwardChip label="from" when="Lecture 2" lec="lec01" k="L-0011">the building blocks on a Medium-like blog</ForwardChip> [[L-0011]]

Because everyone kept asking what Instagram ran, companies that started after 2012 adopted a stack like it. That is an observation from experience, not a survey: pick almost any startup, especially in India, and this stack is very prevalent. [[L-0008]]

</Section>

<Section id="s1-2" no="1.2" title="How stacks spread, and what comes next" crumb="1.2 Stacks spread"
  dek="One company grows massively, reveals its stack, and everyone after it copies the stack without asking why."
  meta="About 3 min · class 00:01–00:06">

Pick any company founded between 2008 and 2011 and look at its stack. In the lecture’s view, a pattern shows up: every few years a revolutionary tech stack appears, everyone jumps onto that bandwagon, and it is very easy to spot. [[L-0005]]

Those years were PHP: PHP, MySQL and Memcache. The reason was Facebook. It was the company that saw massive growth, so everyone asked what it ran and built their own companies on the same stack; colleges then taught PHP, MySQL and the LAMP stack. It was very widely copied, though far from universally: Instagram itself chose Django. Facebook stayed on PHP at enormous scale: in 2010 it served over 400 billion PHP page views a month, and rather than leave PHP it built HipHop, which turns PHP into optimised C++ and cut its web servers’ CPU use by about half <Cite href="https://engineering.fb.com/2010/02/02/developer-tools/hiphop-for-php-move-fast/">engineering.fb.com</Cite>. [[L-0006]]

The lecture’s advice is to always remember the pattern, because it happens every time. One company sees massive growth; everyone keeps asking what its stack is; it reveals it, and every company starting after that jumps on the bandwagon. Nobody thinks out of the box and asks whether they really need it: “if they did it, we can also do the same.” From 2008 to 2012 Facebook made PHP and MySQL famous, and Instagram made the architecture in Fig 1·a famous. [[L-0010]]

<RuleBox rule="Keep an eye on the stack of ==whoever grows fastest==, then ask whether you really need it." k="L-0010 L-0014">

One company sees massive growth and reveals its stack; everyone starting after it copies that stack because “if they did it, we can too”, without asking whether they need it. Watching those shifts as they happen puts you ahead. Copying them blindly does not.

<WatchTheWave />

</RuleBox>

The next shift is due, as a hedged prediction rather than a fact: it has been 10 or 11 years since the industry last revamped its stack, so within the next five years a new one should arrive, maybe based on Rust. It could be a language, a whole stack, or a set of libraries that make things simpler. [[L-0012]]

Go was supposed to be that stack, but it “failed to capture the market”, as an opinion rather than a measurement: it is still going strong, but it is not the default choice for a day-zero architecture. For scale, in Stack Overflow’s 2025 survey 16.4% of all respondents used Go, against 57.9% for Python and 18.9% for PHP <Cite href="https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/technology">survey.stackoverflow.co</Cite>. [[L-0013]]

ChatGPT is one of the recent companies with that kind of massive growth, but it is an AI company, and its backend is expected to be Python: the same stack, not a new one. OpenAI doesn’t publish its full stack, so take that as an educated guess. [[L-0015]]

Startups get their edge from a peculiar power: they are vulnerable yet invincible, because they can move fast and break things. That is an opinion, held without enthusiasm (“not a big fan of it”), but it works. [[L-0016]]

If you just work at a company, you use whatever stack it has. The advice: keeping an eye on the stack of whichever company is growing massively is what puts you in the hot seat, ahead of other people in building. [[L-0014]]

Any four of: Django, the backend that answers requests (scaling the API tier); Celery with RabbitMQ or Redis passing messages (asynchronous processing and communication); Redis or memcached (caching); PostgreSQL, the transactional database, and Cassandra, the non-relational one (the database). All of it ran on AWS.

  </RecallItem>
  <RecallItem q="Why did so many 2008–2011 companies run PHP and MySQL, and what does this chapter’s rule say to do instead of copying?">

Because Facebook ran it and grew massively, everyone asked what it used and built on the same stack. The rule: keep an eye on the stack of whoever grows fastest, and then ask whether you really need it, instead of assuming that what worked for them is right for you.

  </RecallItem>
  <RecallItem q="A new default stack is predicted. What is the evidence, and how sure is the prediction?">

Only the rhythm of the past: it has been 10 or 11 years since the last revamp, so one is due within about five years, “maybe based on Rust”. It is a hedged prediction, not a measurement. Go was the last candidate and, in that opinion, did not become the day-zero default.
