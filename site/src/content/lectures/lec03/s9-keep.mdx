  wrong="Not for a bank: roughly sorted means some IDs can come out of order across machines. That’s fine for a photo feed, not for money.">

Instagram doesn’t mind slight disorder; a bank does. You can’t have strict order and unlimited distributed throughput together, so pick the one your use case needs.

</PauseAndThink>

A social media app like Instagram doesn’t much care if a few IDs come out of order. A financial application listing a customer’s transactions does: it needs the strict order in which the transactions happened. So a bank wouldn’t use Snowflake IDs. Auto-increment is the way to go for it. [[L-0295]]

The reason is the one from [§6.5](#s6-5): Snowflake does not guarantee strict monotonicity, so some IDs can come out of order. Where you need strict order, use auto-increment. ([§6.5](#s6-5) also notes the one caveat: auto-increment hands out increasing values, but concurrent transactions can commit out of ID order.) [[L-0294]]

That has a price. Where strict order is required, as in banks, you lower your throughput expectations: one sequence in one place can’t also scale out the way Snowflake does. [[L-0314]] You cannot guarantee monotonicity in a distributed setup, as [§3.7](#s3-7) and [§6.5](#s6-5) showed; the other systems take rough sortability and the throughput that comes with it. [[L-0313]]

The class deferred the question from [§3.1](#s3-1), resolving conflicts by ID when IDs come from many machines, and did not come back to it. [[L-0090]] What this chapter does give you is the deciding factor, and the inference from here is this page’s own: an ID that must say which write came first needs strict order, and strict order means a single sequence and lower throughput; if rough order is enough, a time-first scheme from this lecture may do.

</Section>

### Habits this lecture keeps coming back to [[rail: Habits]]

1. When you see a collision, add the thing that led to it, then look for a cheaper stand-in ([§2.2](#s2-2)). [[L-0016]]
2. When a machine recovers, start from the safest value; always think correctness first ([§2.6](#s2-6)). [[L-0048]]
3. Put time on the leftmost side, so that as time moves the number grows ([§3.3](#s3-3)). [[L-0060]]
4. Everything is a trade-off: you get something and lose something. So challenge each requirement: integers? random? monotonic? ([§3.8](#s3-8)). [[L-0085 L-0086]]
5. Batch your calls; in the lecture’s view batching is a big lever for performance ([§5.2](#s5-2)). [[L-0103]]
6. Keep IDs small enough that your indexes, at least the hot part, fit in RAM ([§4.3](#s4-3)). [[L-0136]]
7. Understand the context, build only what you need, keep it simple: simple systems scale ([§6.7](#s6-7)). [[L-0245]]
8. Know how your database stores your data on disk ([§8.5](#s8-5)). [[L-0249]]

<MarginNote title="Build them all" why="skip unless you want a project" k="L-0007">

Implement every scheme on this page. None of them is fancy, and you will have fun. Building them is what gives the deep understanding: why each one works, its advantages and disadvantages, what logical partitions are for, and why dump and load matter. When you have built your own, Meituan’s open-source Leaf is a good reference: it offers both models from this lecture, a database-backed “segment” (range) mode and a Snowflake mode taken from Twitter’s algorithm, which needs ZooKeeper <Cite href="https://github.com/Meituan-Dianping/Leaf">github.com</Cite>.

</MarginNote>
