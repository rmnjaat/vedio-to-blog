<Section id="s6-5" no="6.5" title="Where the ring hurts: close nodes, hot spots, virtual nodes" crumb="6.5 Weak spots"
  dek="You don’t choose where nodes land, or where keys pile up. The usual fix, virtual nodes, has a price."
  meta="About 6 min · Q&A 02:32–02:39, 02:51–02:52">

The ring is not perfect. Two weaknesses come straight out of letting a hash function place things.

First, nodes can land too close together on the ring. That is a real disadvantage of consistent hashing, because placement is not in your control: the hash of a node’s id decides it. [[L-0328]]

Second, keys can pile up in one part of the ring. Consistent hashing suffers from hot partitions too. Being hash-based, it hands control to the hash function and asks it to spread keys uniformly enough, the best it can. If your key distribution is bad and your hash function is bad, you won’t see that uniform spread. You see hotspots instead, one node much hotter than the others, and then you add one more node just to try to dilute it. [[L-0330]] Hot partitions are a problem of their own. <ForwardChip when="a later week" k="L-0264">hot partitions, solved when the course designs S3</ForwardChip>

So why do companies still use it? Because it makes scaling up and down simple enough. And the hash range is long, 2<sup>120</sup> as it was put here (2<sup>128</sup> earlier), so everyone is playing by the odds. [[L-0331]] A longer range doesn’t even things out by itself, though: imbalance comes from having few points on the ring, whatever the ring’s size, which is why Amazon’s Dynamo added virtual nodes <Cite href="https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2007/10/amazons_dynamo.html">Dynamo paper</Cite>. [[L-0331]]

Nor is the ring right for every job. There is a case where consistent hashing performs very badly and should never be used, and a different technique works best there. <ForwardChip when="a later week" k="L-0276">where consistent hashing fails, and what to use instead</ForwardChip> [[L-0276]]

**Virtual nodes** are what nearly every explanation of consistent hashing reaches for next. Divide the ring into many small virtual parts (the question in class put it at “10 or 20 or something”) and map those to the physical nodes. A fair worry follows: the logic of consistent hashing is to minimise data transfer, and now each server’s chunks sit at places on the ring that aren’t next to each other. If one server goes down, a lot of scattered chunks have to move, instead of one transfer to a single neighbour. That worry is exactly the disadvantage of virtual nodes. [[L-0332]] They bring back a lot of network transfer, copying from many places instead of one, which is precisely what you adopted consistent hashing to reduce. [[L-0336]]

<SourceNote href="https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2007/10/amazons_dynamo.html" site="Dynamo paper · cassandra.apache.org" k="L-0332 L-0336">

Virtual nodes don’t increase the total data transferred when a node joins or leaves: it is still about 1/N of the data. They change where it comes from, many nodes in parallel instead of one neighbour. Dynamo treats that as a benefit: when a node fails, its load “is evenly dispersed across the remaining available nodes”, and a new node “accepts a roughly equivalent amount of load from each of the other available nodes”. The real costs are operational: more streams to coordinate, and Cassandra notes a higher probability of an outage with many tokens <Cite href="https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html">cassandra.apache.org</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

<Figure id="fig-6g" no="Fig 6·g" k="L-0332 L-0334 L-0336"
  caption="Where a new node’s data comes from. With one token per node, one copy from one neighbour; with virtual nodes, small slices from many nodes. The two readings under the drawing are this chapter’s and Dynamo’s.">
  <Desk><VirtualNodes /></Desk>
  <Phone><VirtualNodes layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Think about it operationally. When cache 5 joined in [6.4](#s6-4), one quick snapshot of cache 1 loaded it. With virtual nodes, a new node is placed at many places on the ring, so its data has to be loaded from many other nodes. That is operationally challenging: you might miss something, and if transferring from any one of those nodes is slow, it slows down the whole process. [[L-0334]] Cassandra’s documentation lists the same kind of costs for many tokens per node: more neighbours per token range, a higher probability of an outage, and more repair operations. Since its 3.x versions, a deterministic token allocator gives balance with far fewer tokens than the old default of 256 <Cite href="https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html">cassandra.apache.org</Cite>. [[L-0334]]

Hence the verdict here: adding virtual nodes is not a good idea for solving the hot partition problem in a stateful use case like this cache. Companies still do it, and there are hacks around it. [[L-0335]]

<SourceNote href="https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2007/10/amazons_dynamo.html" site="Dynamo paper · cassandra.apache.org" k="L-0335">

Virtual nodes are a trade-off for stateful systems, not a mistake: better balance and parallel rebuilds, which is why Dynamo and Cassandra, both stateful stores, use them, against more complex data movement and more nodes involved in each range <Cite href="https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html">cassandra.apache.org</Cite>. Dynamo uses virtual nodes so that a failed node’s load “is evenly dispersed across the remaining available nodes”, and a new node takes roughly equal load from each existing node.

</SourceNote>

It is not the worst option, just bad enough. As long as you are operationally okay with that amount of network transfer, it is fine. The lecture’s preference is to keep things operationally simple: copy one node, bring the server up, done, because simple systems scale. [[L-0337]]

To see the cost for yourself, think it through operationally. How would you copy the data? From how many different places, and how many ranges? How complicated does that get? Work it out, and the case against virtual nodes for a stateful application makes itself. [[L-0353]]

<Section id="s6-6" no="6.6" title="The general idea: distributed hash tables" crumb="6.6 DHTs"
  dek="No centre, and minimal data movement when nodes come and go: what we built is one of a family."
  meta="About 4 min · class 02:23–02:27">

Step back: what have we just designed? Our distributed cache is nothing but a **distributed hash table** (DHT). [[L-0304]]

The idea reaches far. As it was put in class, the entire internet is a distributed hash table, and so are the BitTorrent network and the Bitcoin network. [[L-0305]]

<SourceNote href="https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0005.html" site="bittorrent.org (BEP 5)" k="L-0305">

BitTorrent’s trackerless mode does use a DHT, based on Kademlia. The Internet’s routing and Bitcoin’s peer discovery are not DHTs <Cite href="https://developer.bitcoin.org/devguide/p2p_network.html">developer.bitcoin.org</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

A DHT is a very generic concept. Keep the underlying data in memory and it becomes a distributed cache; keep it on storage and it becomes distributed storage. Only the applications you build on top change. [[L-0306]]

Its defining idea is that there is no central entity everyone talks to. A distributed cache can make the same choice and do without a central routing server. [[L-0307]] At internet scale, in a network like BitTorrent, you talk to the nearest node. If it has the data, it responds. Otherwise it forwards the request, or answers with the address of another machine you can go to, and that one may send you further on. The lookup always converges; that is the property of a DHT. [[L-0308]]

<Figure id="fig-6h" no="Fig 6·h" k="L-0307 L-0308 L-0311"
  caption="A distributed hash table: a key’s hash picks a node, with no load balancer in front, and a lookup that starts at the nearest node and is told where to go. Redrawn from the class board at 02:23–02:27.">
  <Desk><DhtFigure /></Desk>
  <Phone><DhtFigure layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Nodes come and go in such a system, and adding or removing one has to cause minimal data movement, even when a server goes down and another takes its place. That is why consistent-hashing-like techniques are used to implement any DHT. [[L-0309]] Put the two properties together and you have the category: a system with no central entity that must do minimal data transfer as nodes go up and down is a DHT. [[L-0310]]

DHTs are everywhere. The board’s list: distributed file systems (our distributed cache counts as one), DNS servers, instant messaging and IRC clients, peer-to-peer networks like BitTorrent, and IPFS. [[L-0311]]

<SourceNote href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_hash_table" site="en.wikipedia.org" k="L-0311">

DHTs *can* be used to build such services (Wikipedia lists DNS, instant messaging and file sharing as possible uses), but deployed DNS is a hierarchical, delegated system, not a DHT, and IRC is client-server. True DHT examples: BitTorrent’s Mainline DHT <Cite href="https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0005.html">BEP 5</Cite>, IPFS and Kad.

</SourceNote>

IPFS, the InterPlanetary File System (ipfs.io), is a distributed file system built on a DHT, where people store data such as blockchain and NFT data. [[L-0312]]

How does a request find its way when no node knows about every other node on the internet? **Kademlia** solves exactly that: no node knows them all, yet the request is routed and converges on the node that has the data. [[L-0314]] In BitTorrent’s Kademlia DHT, a node asks the nodes it knows that are closest to the target. They return either peers or even closer nodes, and the requester “iteratively queries nodes that are closer” until it finds none closer <Cite href="https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0005.html">BEP 5</Cite>: the redirect way from [5.3](#s5-3), repeated. [[L-0314]]

<MarginNote title="Go deeper: Kademlia" why="optional: P2P routing in depth" k="L-0313">

The board pointed to a 45-minute video deep dive into the Kademlia paper, part of a BitTorrent series. The paper takes days to understand (three, in the lecture’s telling), and it is a mind-blowing implementation that the world uses. Not an obligation, but it is a good way to fall for distributed systems. [[L-0313]]

</MarginNote>

<Section id="s6-7" no="6.7" title="Staying up: replicas, copies on the ring, and the AOF" crumb="6.7 Staying up"
  dek="Cache 3 dies. Where is its data? Redundancy across nodes, and a log on disk."
  meta="About 4 min · class 02:27–02:30, board 02:32">

Cache 3 dies. Where is its data now?

For a cache, high availability starts from the same basic concept as for persistent storage (which may differ in the details): add replicas. Every node has a replica, and if the node goes down, the replica takes its place. It comes at a cost, maintaining that other node, but if availability matters to you, you pay it. [[L-0315]]

Otherwise, keep copies of the data on other nodes of the ring. Whatever data is on cache 1, keep one copy on cache 3; whatever is on cache 3, keep one copy on cache 4. If cache 3 goes down, cache 4 has its data and can serve its requests. [[L-0316]]

That is the beauty of the ring: it lets you set a replication factor. With a replication factor of 2, as it was stated here, the node that owns a key stores it and the two nodes immediately after it on the ring also hold the data. [[L-0317]] By the usual count, owner plus the next two is a replication factor of 3: the factor counts every copy. In Dynamo each key is stored on N nodes, the coordinator and its N−1 clockwise successors, and Cassandra’s RF=3 likewise means three replicas <Cite href="https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2007/10/amazons_dynamo.html">Dynamo paper</Cite> <Cite href="https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html">cassandra.apache.org</Cite>. [[L-0317]]

<Figure id="fig-6i" no="Fig 6·i" k="L-0315 L-0316 L-0317"
  caption="Two ways to survive a node: a standby replica per node, or copies on the next nodes of the ring. Redrawn and extended from the class board at 02:27–02:28.">
  <Desk><SurviveNode /></Desk>
  <Phone><SurviveNode layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

Both are data redundancy, across nodes or across systems, and redundancy is how you get high availability: if a node goes down, you know where to find its data and can reconstruct the node if required. It is standard practice, and it matters even for a cache. [[L-0318]]

Redundancy covers a dead node. A rebooted one needs its data back from disk. Redis has the **AOF**, the append-only file, which the class called Redis’s write-ahead log: every write to Redis is appended to the file, and when the cache rebuilds, after a reboot say, Redis loads everything back from it. [[L-0319]] Strictly, Redis appends commands to the AOF after they are executed, so the AOF is a log of writes rather than a write-ahead log <Cite href="https://redis.io/docs/latest/operate/oss_and_stack/management/persistence/">redis.io</Cite>. [[L-0319]] Two details matter in practice. AOF is off by default (RDB snapshots are the default), and with the default `appendfsync everysec` you can lose about one second of writes; `always` fsyncs on every write but is very slow <Cite href="https://redis.io/docs/latest/operate/oss_and_stack/management/persistence/">redis.io</Cite>. [[L-0319]]

An append-only file is the standard way to ensure durability. (The first word that came out was “reliability”; durability is the right one, and the board’s “Reliability: write-ahead logging” means the same.) [[L-0320]]

The board also had a page for running all of this that was only glimpsed in passing. An orchestrator monitors the uptime of the cache servers through heartbeats and handles failovers, keeping its metadata in a MetaDB; the servers are monitored through Prometheus; and dynamic scaling up and down follows. [[L-0329]]

<Figure id="fig-6j" no="Fig 6·j" max={360} k="L-0329"
  caption="The orchestrator: heartbeats in, failovers out, metadata in a MetaDB, Prometheus watching the servers. Only glimpsed on the board at 02:32; the layout is approximate.">
  <Orchestrator />
</Figure>

That completes the distributed cache. Storage: keep the data on separate nodes, with some logic to determine ownership; hash-based and range-based routing are the two very common ways. [[L-0321]] The single node got the most time, then routing in depth, availability with replicas, and scaling with consistent hashing, where doing minimal data transfer as nodes are added is what matters. [[L-0322]]

<h3 id="s6-check">Check yourself</h3>

Answer in your head, or out loud, before opening each one.

<RecallQuestions>
  <RecallItem k="L-0274 L-0291 L-0292" q="With % N, going from 2 to 3 nodes moved 3 of 6 keys. On the ring, adding cache 5 at position 1 moves which keys?">

Only the keys that hash into the slice cache 5 now covers: after 12, through 0, up to 1. K1 is one of them. They all move from one node, cache 1, the next node clockwise; every other key keeps its owner. On average a ring moves about 1/(N+1) of the keys, against about N/(N+1) for `% N`.

  </RecallItem>
  <RecallItem k="L-0295 L-0297 L-0298" q="Name the two ways to remove a node and what each costs.">

Graceful: snapshot the node, copy its data to the next node clockwise, then take it off the ring. It costs one copy, and there are no misses. Abrupt: the node dies. Its whole range lands on the next node cold, so requests miss and the backend takes the load. Either way, no other key moves.

  </RecallItem>
  <RecallItem k="L-0332 L-0334 L-0335 L-0336" q="Virtual nodes: what does this chapter say they cost, and what does Dynamo say they buy?">

The cost, as argued here: a new or failed node’s data moves from or to many places instead of one neighbour, which is harder to run, can miss something, and is as slow as the slowest source; hence no virtual nodes for a stateful cache. Dynamo’s side: the total moved is still about 1/N, a failed node’s load spreads evenly across the rest, and a new node takes a roughly equal share from each, which gives better balance.

  </RecallItem>
</RecallQuestions>
