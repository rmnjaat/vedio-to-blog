<Section id="s3-5" no="3.5" title="Message streams" crumb="3.5 Streams"
  dek="Give each team its own consumer of the same event, and let the messages stay."
  meta="About 4 min · class 01:14–01:22">

The worker in 3.4 failed because one consumer did two teams’ jobs. The fix is to give each job its own consumer of the same `ON_PUBLISH` event. A queue can’t do that, because a message one consumer takes goes to no other. **Message streams** solve the problem on a different principle, and the two you will meet most are Kafka and Kinesis. [[L-0168 L-0162]]

The difference is who the consumers are. In a queue they are **homogeneous**: identical workers running the same code, and any one of them can take any message. In a stream they are **heterogeneous**: the counter service and the search service both read every `ON_PUBLISH` message, and each does something different with it. [[L-0170]]

That only works if reading a message doesn’t remove it. Kafka keeps the messages buffered until the retention period is over. Consumers don’t pull messages out of a stream. They iterate over them, each keeping its own place, and no consumer ever deletes one. [[L-0172 L-0173]]

So what does remove them? A deletion policy that you set: keep messages for 40 days, or 60, or 100. That is your call, not the consumers’. If you don’t set one, you get the default: Kafka’s broker keeps messages for 7 days (`log.retention.hours=168`), and a topic can use compaction instead, keeping only the latest message per key (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/configuration/broker-configs/">kafka.apache.org</Cite>). Kinesis keeps records for 24 hours by default, and up to 365 days at extra cost (<Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/streams/latest/dev/kinesis-extended-retention.html">docs.aws.amazon.com</Cite>). Long retention is something you configure. [[L-0174]]

<AnimationClip clip="l01-queue-vs-stream" no="Clip 3·1" k="L-0173 L-0174 L-0175 L-0176"
  lead="Before you play it: Elasticsearch goes down for ten minutes. In which picture does the counter stop too?"
  caption="The same five ON_PUBLISH events go to a queue and to a stream. The queue hands each to one worker, which deletes it. The stream keeps them, and the search and counter consumers each move at their own pace; when Elasticsearch is down only search waits." />

In the stream, the counter never stops. Each consumer type iterates over the messages at its own pace and does its own thing, so search and counter don’t have to handle the same message at the same moment. If the search cluster is down, the counter consumer has nothing to do with it and keeps moving ahead. When search comes back, it carries on from where it stopped. [[L-0175 L-0176]]

In a queue you delete. In a stream you iterate, and, as 3.7 shows, you commit.

Here is Medium with Kafka. On publish, the API server sends one message to Kafka, which persists it. The search service and the counter service each consume it at their own pace: one indexes the story into Elasticsearch, the other runs `total_blogs++` in the database. The search team owns the search codebase and the counter team owns theirs. Nobody is blocked on anybody else’s deploy. [[L-0178]]

  caption="One ON_PUBLISH event, two kinds of consumer. Each service reads the same messages from its own position, and each team owns its own code. Redrawn from the class board at 01:17 and 01:20.">
  <Desk><Fig3f /></Desk>
  <Phone><Fig3f layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

The working rule the class took from this: decouple as much as possible. [[L-0179]]

The lecture also made a bigger claim, and it is an opinion worth hearing as one. Kafka and Kinesis are, in this view, the most powerful tools in day-to-day engineering. Companies have re-architected their whole systems around them because they solve big enterprise problems, and part of what they solve is organisational: managing people is hard, and a stream lets teams work without waiting on each other. [[L-0177]]

<Section id="s3-6" no="3.6" title="Kafka essentials: topics, partitions, consumer groups" crumb="3.6 Kafka essentials"
  dek="What you need to design with Kafka: where a message lands, who reads it, and how far you can scale the readers."
  meta="About 9 min · class 01:21–01:34, Q&A 02:30–02:56">

Kafka is the backbone of a great many modern systems, which is reason enough to know its critical parts. It is also widely misunderstood. Follow one author who publishes two stories in a row on Medium and you will meet every part that matters. [[L-0180]]

### Topics and partitions

Kafka keeps messages in **topics**. Think of a topic as a named queue. When the API server sends a message to Kafka, it sends it to a particular topic, for example `ON_PUBLISH`, and Kafka stores it under that topic. [[L-0181]]

Each topic is split into some number of **partitions**, and different topics can have different numbers of them. [[L-0182]]

When the producer sends a message, it also gives a **partitioning key**. Kafka hashes the key, and the hash decides which partition stores the message. [[L-0183]]

Make `user_id` the key and every story one author publishes lands in the same partition. It is the same idea as sharding the database by user ID in 2.4: hash the ID and the hash picks the place. [[L-0184]]

<Figure id="fig-3g" no="Fig 3·g" k="L-0181 L-0182 L-0183 L-0184 L-0188 L-0201"
  caption="The ON_PUBLISH topic with three partitions. The key is hashed, so both of u7’s stories land in p1. Redrawn from the class board at 01:21–01:24.">
  <Desk><Fig3g /></Desk>
  <Phone><Fig3g layout="phone" /></Phone>
</Figure>

That placement is what gives you ordering, and only within a partition. If the author publishes story 1 and then story 2, both are in the same partition, and whoever reads it sees story 1 first. Across partitions there is no guarantee at all. The producer side keeps its half of the promise through idempotence, on by default: with it switched off and several requests in flight, a retry can reorder records (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/configuration/producer-configs/">kafka.apache.org</Cite>). [[L-0188]]

Since messages are spread over partitions, it is natural to ask how the consumers coordinate a global order. They don’t. Kafka has no global ordering. [[L-0201]]

### Consumer groups

One consumer could index every story, but you usually run several of the same type, three search consumers for example, to get the indexing done as fast as possible. The counter consumers read the same topic separately. [[L-0185]]

A set of consumers of one type reading a topic together is a **consumer group**. Kafka decides which consumer in the group reads which partition, and you don’t have to do anything. [[L-0186]]

<MarginNote title="Who actually computes the assignment" why="skip unless you configure Kafka clients" k="L-0186">

From the developer’s side, “Kafka does it” is right. Under the hood, Kafka 4.0 made a new consumer group protocol (KIP-848) generally available, which moves the assignment to the broker and removes “stop-the-world” rebalances. Clients opt in with `group.protocol=consumer`; the default is still the classic protocol as of Kafka 4.3 (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2025/03/18/apache-kafka-4.0.0-release-announcement/">kafka.apache.org</Cite>, <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/43/configuration/consumer-configs/">consumer configs</Cite>).

</MarginNote>

A consumer doesn’t choose a partition. It starts and reads, and Kafka manages which partition it reads from, which is what makes consumers easy to write. [[L-0359]]

A common misreading says each consumer in a group subscribes to a single partition and has to learn about the others somehow. You never subscribe a consumer to only one partition. With fewer consumers than partitions, Kafka simply gives some consumers more than one. The ideal is N consumers for N partitions, but fewer is fine, and there is still no global ordering either way. [[L-0202]]

With three partitions and three consumers in the group, each gets one: consumer 1 reads partition 1, consumer 2 reads partition 2, consumer 3 reads partition 3. [[L-0187]]

With a single consumer, that consumer gets messages from all three. It isn’t a strict round robin: the consumer is assigned all three partitions and fetches from them in its poll loop (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/consumer/KafkaConsumer.html">KafkaConsumer docs</Cite>). [[L-0192]]

<PauseAndThink k="L-0187 L-0189 L-0190 L-0336 L-0361"
  q="<code>ON_PUBLISH</code> has 3 partitions. You start 5 search consumers in one group. (a) What do consumers 4 and 5 do? (b) Why not let two consumers share a partition?"
  choices={[{ label: 'They share the load with the others' }, { label: 'They sit idle', correct: true }]}
  right="Right: in one group, the partition count caps the busy consumers."
  wrong="Extra consumers don’t split a partition; they wait:">

(a) They sit idle. Kafka gives each partition to exactly one consumer in a group, so the parallelism of one consumer type equals the partition count.

(b) Kafka tracks a group’s progress in a partition as one position, and it promises order within a partition. If two consumers read one partition, Kafka would have to track which consumer read which message while still keeping the order, and that isn’t possible in this design. It is a design choice rather than a law of nature: Kafka 4.2’s share groups make exactly that trade (see the note below). To go wider today, forward into SQS and fan out (Fig 3·i).

</PauseAndThink>

<SourceNote href="https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-932%3A+Queues+for+Kafka" site="KIP-932" k="L-0361">

The reasoning is right in spirit: one offset per partition per group, plus ordering within a partition, keeps the tracking cheap. It is a design choice, though, not an impossibility. Kafka’s share groups (KIP-932, early access in 4.0, production-ready in 4.2; 4.3 is current) let several consumers read one partition with per-record acknowledgements, giving up strict order. Partition count still caps *ordered* parallelism. See also the <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/design/design/">Kafka design docs</Cite> and the <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2026/02/17/apache-kafka-4.2.0-release-announcement/">4.2 release notes</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

  caption="As many consumers as partitions. Consumers 4 and 5 get nothing. A second consumer group reads the same topic with its own cap of three. Redrawn from the class board at 01:24.">

### The partition cap

This is the biggest limitation of Kafka: how many consumers can work in parallel depends on the number of partitions in the topic. With 3 partitions you can have at most 3 consumers of one type. That holds for classic consumer groups; Kafka 4.2’s share groups relax it, at the cost of ordering (see the note above, and the <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2026/02/17/apache-kafka-4.2.0-release-announcement/">4.2 announcement</Cite>). [[L-0190]]

Add a 4th and a 5th consumer to that group and they get no messages. [[L-0189]]

The cap applies to each consumer type separately. One topic can have N types of consumer, such as analytics, counter and search, and each can have up to three. [[L-0191]]

So how many partitions should a topic have? It depends on how fast you need to consume. Pick the count from the consumption rate you expect, and size it up front: adding partitions later doesn’t move existing data, keyed messages may start landing in a different partition, and Kafka can’t reduce a topic’s partition count (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/operations/basic-kafka-operations/">kafka.apache.org</Cite>). [[L-0207]]

Everything here holds for Kinesis too. Kafka has partitions, Kinesis has **shards**: the same idea under a different name, with nearly the same architecture underneath, and every message stream works this way with its own API and vocabulary. One difference worth knowing is that each Kinesis shard has a fixed capacity, up to 1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s of writes and 2 MB/s of reads, so the shard count sets both throughput and the maximum parallelism of a consumer application (<Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/streams/latest/dev/service-sizes-and-limits.html">docs.aws.amazon.com</Cite>). Retention differs too, 24 hours by default against Kafka’s 7 days. [[L-0197]]

Kinesis also hides the assignment: when a Kinesis consumer starts, you don’t say which shard it reads. Strictly, it is the Kinesis Client Library that assigns shards for you; with the raw API you name the shard yourself (<Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/streams/latest/dev/shared-throughput-kcl-consumers.html">KCL docs</Cite>). [[L-0360]]

Routing everything for one key to one consumer has a performance side as well. Since all the messages for one author arrive at one place, that consumer can cache what it needs locally and probably write in bulk. (The class mentioned this in passing, and the recording is unclear on the details.) [[L-0313]]

### Beyond the partition cap [[rail: Kafka → SQS]]

Sometimes one consumer type needs far more workers than you want partitions, and repartitioning means a rebalance. The way out is to run a few Kafka consumers, say three for three partitions, whose only job is to read each message and push it into a queue such as SQS. Then let 100 workers on SQS do the heavy lifting. [[L-0336]]

The price is one more component that somebody has to run. [[L-0337]]

Kafka feeding SQS feeding hundreds of workers is very common in notification systems. Sending a notification takes far longer than consuming the event, so you need more fan-out than the partitions allow. [[L-0338]]

<MarginNote title="Is there a Kafka “broadcast” feature?" why="skip unless you heard this claim elsewhere" k="L-0339 L-0340 L-0398">

Kafka is traditionally described as a “dumb” broker. It doesn’t fetch from producers or push to consumers: producers send messages and consumers pull them. [[L-0340]]

In class, a “broadcasting” feature came up that would push messages to every consumer group of a topic, which would be an attempt at real-time pub/sub. Nobody was sure of its advantages, and it was noted as something to look up. [[L-0339]]

At the end of class it came back as a point about tools changing: Kafka now has a broadcast capability that covers a kind of real-time pub/sub, so you can’t simply say Kafka doesn’t do real-time pub/sub. [[L-0398]]

<SourceNote href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/design/design/" site="kafka.apache.org" k="L-0398">

No Kafka feature called “broadcast” appears in the Apache Kafka docs or KIPs, checked through 4.3. That is absence of evidence, not proof. Delivering every message to every consumer group has always been Kafka’s basic model. The notable recent change runs the other way: share groups add queue semantics (<Cite href="https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-932%3A+Queues+for+Kafka">KIP-932</Cite>).

</SourceNote>

</MarginNote>

<Section id="s3-7" no="3.7" title="Committing, and why messages come back" crumb="3.7 Committing"
  dek="How a consumer tells Kafka how far it got, and what happens when it dies before saying so."
  meta="About 4 min · class 01:27–01:37, Q&A 02:56–02:57">

The counter consumer has processed four `ON_PUBLISH` messages when its machine dies. When a new one starts, where does it begin?

From its last **commit**. After reading some messages, a consumer commits to Kafka. This isn’t a database commit: it is Kafka recording, per consumer group, how far that group has read. A consumer that restarts resumes from that point. The record is cheap, because a group’s position in a partition is one integer, the next offset to read, and the same mechanism lets a consumer rewind to an older offset to reprocess data (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/design/design/">kafka.apache.org</Cite>). [[L-0193]]

<p class="lead-in">Step through what happens when the crash comes between processing and committing:</p>

The consumer crashed, not Kafka. It had processed four messages but not committed them, so on resume it started again from the last commit and those four messages came back. [[L-0194]]

Out of the box, Kafka guarantees **at-least-once** delivery, not exactly-once. It has started offering a kind of exactly-once, but not a true one, and exactly-once is reached by other means. [[L-0195]]

<SourceNote href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/design/design/" site="kafka.apache.org" k="L-0195">

At-least-once by default is right. Exactly-once does exist *inside* Kafka, since version 0.11: transactional producers plus `read_committed` consumers, as Kafka Streams uses for Kafka-to-Kafka processing. (Committing before processing gives at-most-once instead.) The real limit is side effects outside Kafka, such as a database `count++`: exactly-once there “generally requires cooperation” from that system. See also the <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/configuration/producer-configs/">producer configs</Cite>.

</SourceNote>

So, in the simple view, your consumers must be fine with seeing the same messages again. For `total_blogs++` a repeat means a double count: an author with 74 essays publishes once, and the profile can say 76. Keeping a shared counter correct is exactly what §4 is about. [[L-0196]]

You can commit after every message, but that is a network call per message. Committing in batches is better. [[L-0208]]

It is tempting to think a stream commits for you, since plenty of consumer code never calls `commit`. Commits are never magic. Either your code commits, per message or per batch, or you have configured auto-commit or a periodic commit. The Java client, for one, auto-commits by default every 5 seconds, and that is at-least-once only if you finish processing each `poll()` batch before the next poll; otherwise records can be skipped (<Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/configuration/consumer-configs/">consumer configs</Cite>, <Cite href="https://kafka.apache.org/42/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/consumer/KafkaConsumer.html">KafkaConsumer docs</Cite>). [[L-0214]]

One line to remember: in a queue you delete; in a stream you commit. If you don’t commit, you start again from the last checkpoint. [[L-0213]]

</Section>

<Section id="s3-8" no="3.8" title="Choosing: queue, stream, or broadcast" crumb="3.8 Choosing"
  dek="One question picks the broker for most jobs: how many different things happen to each message? Then cost, ordering, and a third option."
  meta="About 8 min · class 01:30–01:36, Q&A 02:27–03:14">

Medium wants to send a welcome email when someone signs up. Does that need Kafka?

Start from what queues and streams share. Both are a buffer that holds messages, and both have consumers that go through the messages at their own pace. [[L-0203]]

The difference is the number of things that happen to each message. If there is only one operation to do on a message, use a queue. That is the best way to look at it. A welcome email is one operation, so it goes on a queue. [[L-0204]]

Put another way, a queue has one group of homogeneous consumers, and whoever reads a message gets it. If you need more than one consumer group, you need a stream. [[L-0209]]

### Why not use streams for everything?

Cost. AWS bills SQS per message and Kinesis for the time it is running, since a stream is infrastructure that runs all the time. For smaller workloads, choose SQS rather than pay for a stream. In detail: SQS bills per request, counting each 64 KB chunk of payload as one request, with the first million requests a month free. Kinesis in provisioned mode bills per shard-hour plus PUT payload units; its on-demand modes bill per GB written and read, one of them with a per-stream hourly charge (<Cite href="https://aws.amazon.com/sqs/pricing/">SQS pricing</Cite>, <Cite href="https://aws.amazon.com/kinesis/data-streams/pricing/">Kinesis pricing</Cite>). [[L-0205]]

Always think about cost. Part of your job is to save the organisation money, and however fancy a design is, if it isn’t cost-efficient it won’t see the light of day. That is the class’s rule. [[L-0206]]

Kafka will still turn up in almost every system designed in this course. Whenever we delegate and need several types of consumer, it is the default choice. [[L-0198]]

That doesn’t make it salt to sprinkle on every design. Kafka and consistent hashing are both very stupid solutions in some cases. Know their strengths and weaknesses so the choice is a sane one. [[L-0200]]

There is one place where Kafka fails miserably, where it is the worst choice you could make. <ForwardChip when="a later week" /> [[L-0199]]

### Broadcast: SNS

AWS has a third service in this family, and it is neither a queue nor a stream. **SNS** is a notification service: you send it data, and it broadcasts the data to everyone subscribed. [[L-0303]]

So AWS’s map reads: SQS is a queue, Kinesis is a stream, SNS is pub/sub. [[L-0304]]

Standard SNS doesn’t keep messages for later readers, a point raised in class: a subscriber that wasn’t listening misses them. [[L-0305]]

Put SNS in front of SQS and you can mimic Kafka: SNS fans each message out into one SQS queue per consumer type, so several kinds of consumer receive the same messages. [[L-0306 L-0311]]

<Figure id="fig-3k" no="Fig 3·k" sheet k="L-0303 L-0306 L-0307 L-0311" wide={false}
  caption="SNS pushes a copy of each message into one SQS queue per consumer type. Standard SNS keeps no history; SNS FIFO topics can now archive and replay. New figure.">
  <Fig3k />
</Figure>

What you lose is history. Kafka keeps old messages so a consumer can go back over them; standard SNS plus SQS can’t. The gap has narrowed: SNS FIFO topics can now archive messages for up to 365 days and replay them to subscriptions (<Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sns/latest/dg/message-archiving-and-replay-topic-owner.html">docs.aws.amazon.com</Cite>). [[L-0307]]

Ordering and sharding are key criteria when choosing between SQS and Kinesis. With sharding, all messages for one ID land in one shard and are consumed by one consumer, so you control how they reach the database. [[L-0312]]

<MarginNote title="From practice: SQS to Kinesis" why="skip unless you’re on SQS today" k="L-0310">

One team built on SQS. With no ordering and no sharding, several messages for the same database object arrived together and several consumers picked them up at the same time. Even with retries, processing kept failing, so the team re-architected and moved from SQS to Kinesis, where everything for one key goes to one shard and one consumer. [[L-0310]]

<SourceNote href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSSimpleQueueService/latest/SQSDeveloperGuide/sqs-fifo-queues.html" site="docs.aws.amazon.com" k="L-0310">

Only SQS *standard* queues lack ordering. SQS FIFO queues keep strict order per `MessageGroupId`: while one message of a group is in flight, later messages of that group wait. They also deduplicate. That is the per-key guarantee the team moved to Kinesis for (<Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSSimpleQueueService/latest/SQSDeveloperGuide/standard-queues.html">standard queues</Cite>, <Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSSimpleQueueService/latest/SQSDeveloperGuide/sqs-visibility-timeout.html">visibility timeout</Cite>).

</SourceNote>

</MarginNote>

### What streams don’t replace

Streams don’t replace distributed transactions. In the database and Elasticsearch example, a stream works because two independent processes do two independent things with the same message, each at its own pace. Some cases still need a distributed transaction. [[L-0362]]

If two writes to two different stores must be strongly consistent, there is no option other than a distributed transaction. The outbox pattern from 3.4 is the usual practical way around it, and it buys eventual consistency, not strong. [[L-0363]]

### Tools converge [[rail: Tools converge]]

The lines between these tools keep moving. RabbitMQ is mostly known as a message queue, but it has persistent queues that cover the stream use case: several consumers over persistent data. [[L-0395]]

<SourceNote href="https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/streams" site="rabbitmq.com" k="L-0395">

Plain durable queues are still destructive: a message is removed once it is acked. What covers the stream use case is RabbitMQ Streams, added in 3.9 (2021): an append-only, replicated log with non-destructive reads and replay from the beginning, an offset or a timestamp (<Cite href="https://github.com/rabbitmq/rabbitmq-server/releases/tag/v3.9.0">3.9.0 release</Cite>).

</SourceNote>

Databases are the same. MongoDB originally didn’t want ACID properties and now supports them, so “MongoDB doesn’t support ACID”, or “NoSQL doesn’t support ACID”, is wrong. [[L-0397]]

Databases are converging and starting to look alike: MySQL and Postgres store JSON, MongoDB has ACID transactions, wide-column stores offer some transactional guarantees (Cassandra’s are lightweight transactions, compare-and-set, not full ACID), and Hudi supports transactions. Building features has become easy, so everyone does everything (<Cite href="https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/json.html">MySQL JSON</Cite>, <Cite href="https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/datatype-json.html">Postgres JSON</Cite>, <Cite href="https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/guarantees.html">Cassandra guarantees</Cite>, <Cite href="https://hudi.apache.org/docs/concurrency_control/">Hudi</Cite>). [[L-0399]]

So avoid generic statements like “X doesn’t support Y”. There is no hard and fast rule; check what the tool can do today. [[L-0396]]

<RecallQuestions>
  <RecallItem q="Why did moving the Elasticsearch write into its own consumer fix the 75/74 mismatch, and what problem does it not fix?">

Each write now belongs to its own consumer of the same message, at its own position. A crash or retry in one consumer re-runs only its own write, never the other team’s, and search being down doesn’t stop the counter. It doesn’t make anything exactly-once: each consumer is still at-least-once, so the counter can count a story twice, and the two stores agree only eventually. Strong consistency across them still needs a distributed transaction.

  </RecallItem>
  <RecallItem q="Your ON_PUBLISH topic has 6 partitions and you run 10 search consumers. How many are busy, and what can you do about it?">

Six are busy and four sit idle: in a consumer group each partition goes to exactly one consumer. To go wider, add partitions (better sized up front, since adding them later remaps keys and you can’t remove them), or put a few forwarders in front of SQS and fan out to as many workers as you need. Kafka 4.2’s share groups are another option if you can give up ordering.

  </RecallItem>
  <RecallItem q="A queue consumer crashes mid-message. In SQS, what makes the message come back, and what must your code do to say “done”?">

The visibility timeout. While a consumer works on a message, SQS hides it from the others; if nobody deletes it before the timeout expires, it becomes visible again and another consumer picks it up. Your code says “done” by deleting the message after processing it. Not deleting it is the failure signal.

  </RecallItem>
</RecallQuestions>
