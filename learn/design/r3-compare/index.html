Lecture 1 · design round 3 · working prototypes

Five ways to learn "Scaling the database"

Each one is a working build of the same slice of the lecture, made from the 54-site research, not from earlier rounds. Open each one and use it: click, step, flip, turn the dial. All five carry every scaling item from the lecture (72 items), with fact-check corrections and sources.

Tell me which one to take forward, or which parts of several to combine.

01

Living Map

Medium's architecture fills the screen. A 22-step tour changes it from one box to sharded databases, one sentence per step. Click any part for its card.

How to try it

  1. Use ← → or the round buttons; drag the bar at the bottom to jump between chapters
  2. Click a part on the map (the small +) to open its card
  3. Try the sliders inside the cards: replicas needed, replica lag, users moved when a shard is added
Open Living Map

Text on screen: About 5% of the screen is text on the map, 17% with a card open

Works
Builds the picture of the whole system the lecture is really teaching
Calm; easy to come back to as revision
Weak
Some lines cross on the replica and shard steps; a dot sits on the CDN label
Cards are still dense
02

On-Call at Medium

You are the engineer on call. Five incidents, from the 09:00 newsletter spike to sharding. Pick a move, watch the system react, then read the debrief.

How to try it

  1. Pick a, b or c; wrong moves play out too, then you can retry
  2. Open the debrief cards after each outcome
  3. Tabs at the top jump between incidents; 'Shift log' shows what each one taught
Open On-Call at Medium

Text on screen: 6–10% on desktop, 13% on phone

Works
Guessing before the answer is built in; the class's real wrong turns become moves with consequences
Most gripping of the five
Weak
Incident details are invented (labelled as examples); the simulation is scripted
Debrief cards are dense; the 15-replica drawing is busy on phones
03

Episodes

An illustrated episode, 'Too Many Readers'. Postgres, the API servers, the load balancer, replicas and shards are characters. Every panel flips to an engineer view.

How to try it

  1. Click or press Enter on a panel to flip it to the engineer view
  2. 'Engineer view on every panel' flips them all
  3. Ends with the real numbers and three recall questions
Open Episodes

Text on screen: 3–6% with panels unflipped, about 19% with two flipped

Works
The most memorable and the most distinctive; nothing in system design teaching looks like it
Lowest text of the five
Weak
On desktop a flipped panel stretches its row and leaves gaps
Characters are stiff next to real illustration; could feel junior to some readers
04

Plates

22 full-screen plates, one idea each, in six colour themes. Each has one statement and one figure; detail opens behind '+' buttons. Five plates ask you to guess first.

How to try it

  1. Scroll or swipe plate by plate, or use the arrows bottom right
  2. Press the '+' buttons to open detail
  3. 'Next step' walks through each figure
Open Plates

Text on screen: 0–8% running text; statements 5–11%

Works
Calmest and the easiest to read on a phone
Big statements make the main idea of each plate unmissable
Weak
On desktop the detail panel covers its figure
Can still feel like a slide deck
05

Scale Dial

One dial from 10 to 1 million requests a second. As you turn it, Medium's architecture redraws and four gauges move: database CPU, connections, replica lag, cost.

How to try it

  1. Drag the dial at the bottom, or use arrow keys and Page Up/Down
  2. Switch reads:writes between 50:50, 90:10 and 99:1 and watch the stops move
  3. 'Open stop' shows broke / change / cost in a short card deck
Open Scale Dial

Text on screen: 6–12% on desktop, 9% on phone

Works
One unforgettable control; shows why each change happens when it does
The read:write switch makes trade-offs tangible
Weak
The toy model's assumed numbers are the most prominent ones (they are labelled)
Fits scaling and caching better than concurrency or protocols

What I'd do next

These can combine. A strong full lecture could use the Living Map as home (the whole system, always visible), On-Call incidents as the exercises at the end of each topic, and an Episode to open each topic.

If you'd rather keep one model throughout, the Living Map or On-Call scale best to all five topics of the lecture.

Known fixes before the full build

  1. Clean up crossing lines and overlapping labels on the maps
  2. Stop panels covering figures on desktop (Plates, Living Map cards)
  3. Tighten dense cards and debriefs
  4. Every invented number stays visibly labelled as an example
